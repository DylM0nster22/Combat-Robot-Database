Titanium Grade 5: The Antweight Armor King

Metals Deep Dive

antweightplastic-antweighttitaniumarmordeep-divemachining

Ti-6Al-4V (Grade 5) earned its reputation as the default premium antweight armor material almost entirely on one number: strength-to-weight ratio. At roughly 4.43 g/cm³ — about 57% the density of steel and only slightly denser than aluminum — it delivers yield strength around 880 MPa, several times aluminum's and comparable to many hardened steels, meaning a thin titanium sheet gives steel-like resistance to penetration and denting at close to aluminum's weight.

Thickness selection in practice: the community has converged on a few standard gauges for antweight use. 0.5mm sheet is thin enough to be springy — it's commonly formed/wrapped around wheels as a flexible guard, or bent into skirts that flex and rebound rather than holding a fixed rigid shape. 0.8mm and 1.0mm are the workhorse thicknesses for flat, rigid armor panels and wedge faces bolted to a frame — stiff enough to hold shape, still light enough to cover most of a 1 lb bot's surface without consuming the whole weight budget. 1.5mm and up starts to eat seriously into the weight budget for a pure antweight and is more typical of beetleweight-adjacent or armor-only-on-critical-faces designs.

Why it's hard to work with: titanium has low thermal conductivity, so heat generated by cutting doesn't dissipate into the material — it concentrates right at the cutting edge, which both damages standard high-speed-steel tooling and can locally anneal or discolor (heat-tint) the titanium itself if pushed too hard. It also work-hardens quickly, meaning a dull or slow-feeding tool makes the surface progressively harder to cut, compounding the problem. Practical hobbyist approaches for thin sheet: a fine-tooth jeweler's saw or Dremel-style abrasive cutoff wheel for one-off cuts, or outsourcing to waterjet/CNC services that already run carbide tooling and coolant for combat-robot-specific titanium cutting. Drilling needs sharp new carbide or cobalt bits, low RPM, high feed pressure, and cutting fluid — a bit that's allowed to rub rather than bite will work-harden the hole surface and can permanently ruin that bit.

The brittleness tradeoff: despite its strength, Ti-6Al-4V is more brittle than a comparable steel (lower ductility/elongation-to-break), so where a steel plate might dent and stay dented, a titanium plate is more likely to develop a visible crack under an equally hard hit — the crack often doesn't propagate immediately to failure, but repeated hits to the same location can eventually punch through. This is why titanium armor is often designed with the highest-probability impact faces getting the material, rather than a uniform full-coverage shell, and why many builders back critical titanium panels with a thin compliant layer (foam or TPU) rather than mounting them rigidly.

Cost reality: raw mill titanium plate is not dramatically expensive per kg in bulk, but the small quantities and pre-cut sheet sizes hobby suppliers sell (FingerTech, Combat Robotics NZ, PalmBeachBots, and similar) carry a significant markup over mill pricing because of cutting, packaging, and shipping small orders — expect to pay well above raw-material commodity pricing for hobby-sized titanium sheet.
