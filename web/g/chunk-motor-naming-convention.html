FPV motor naming: stator diameter, stator height, and why volume predicts torque

Weapon Motor Fundamentals

antweightplastic-antweightnaming-conventionstatorsizing

Every combat-robot weapon motor pulled from the FPV drone world is named with a four-digit code: the first two digits are the stator diameter in millimeters, and the last two are the stator height (stack length) in millimeters. A "2207" motor has a 22mm-diameter, 7mm-tall stator. A "1306" is 13mm diameter, 6mm tall. This is not a marketing number -- it is a direct physical measurement of the laminated iron core the copper is wound around, and it is the single most useful spec for predicting what a motor can do as a weapon drive, more useful than KV alone.

Why volume, not KV, predicts torque and power. Torque in a brushless motor comes from the magnetic force between the stator's electromagnets and the rotor's permanent magnets, multiplied by the radius at which that force acts. A wider stator (bigger diameter) puts the copper further from the shaft, giving a longer moment arm for the same force -- more torque per amp. A taller stator (bigger height) fits more copper per phase and more iron to carry flux without saturating, which raises the current (and therefore torque and sustained power) the motor can handle before overheating or magnetically saturating. Multiply the two together and you get an approximate "stator volume," and torque and continuous power both scale roughly with that volume for motors of similar design and KV.

This is why two motors with the same first two digits can behave very differently: a 2504 (wide, short) is torque-biased and modest on continuous power, good for a belt-reduced drum needing bite; a 2207 (moderate diameter, taller) balances torque and RPM headroom well, which is why it is the antweight bread-and-butter size; a 1408 (narrow, tall for its diameter) gives surprisingly good torque for its 30g weight because the height compensates for the smaller diameter.

Practical rule of thumb for weapon selection: when comparing two motors at the same KV, the one with more stator volume (bigger diameter x height) will produce more torque and tolerate more stall current before overheating or demagnetizing -- so it will spin up a given weapon MOI faster and survive more violent stalls. When comparing motors at different KV, remember that a lower-KV winding of the same stator generally trades top RPM for more torque per amp, which is exactly why the size ladder (11xx through 28xx) also correlates with typical KV ranges dropping as diameter/height increase: 1104-1408 motors commonly run 3000-6000kv, the 18xx-22xx bread-and-butter class runs 1300-2700kv, and 25xx-28xx motors commonly run 800-1900kv for the same voltage classes.
