KV selection for weapon motors: why low KV plus more cells beats high KV plus fewer cells

KV & Motor Constants

antweightplastic-antweightkv-selectionbitetip-speed

KV is the RPM a brushless motor spins per volt applied with no load. It is fixed by how the motor is wound -- more turns of thinner wire gives lower KV and more torque per amp; fewer turns of thicker wire gives higher KV and more RPM per amp, for the same stator. KV alone tells you nothing about power or torque; it only tells you the RPM-to-voltage relationship. Two motors can share a KV and differ enormously in torque if their stator volumes differ.

The community rule -- low KV, high cell count -- comes from bite, not efficiency. For a fixed target tip speed, you can get there with a high-KV motor on a low cell count (say 2S) or a low-KV motor on a higher cell count (say 4S or 6S) reflected through gearing, or with a genuinely lower-KV motor direct-driving a bigger-diameter weapon. What matters in a hit is not tip speed alone but bite: how deeply the weapon's edge engages the opponent's armor before sliding off, which scales with how much torque is available at the RPM the weapon is turning, and with how large/heavy the weapon is relative to its RPM. A small, high-KV, low-torque motor spinning a light, fast weapon tends to skate across armor without digging in ("all speed, no bite"), while a bigger, lower-KV motor spinning a heavier weapon at proportionally lower RPM for the same tip speed captures more material per hit and transfers energy more effectively into denting or ejecting the opponent.

Tip speed targets. The antweight community converges on roughly 60-115 m/s (200-380 ft/s, 130-260 mph) tip speed for most vertical disc and bar spinners, with 200-250 mph (about 90-112 m/s) treated as a sweet spot: fast enough to store real kinetic energy, slow enough to keep spin-up time and current draw reasonable and to preserve bite. Horizontal spinners/bars, which suffer far more aerodynamic drag, are usually tuned toward the lower end of that range or even lower, since a bar or blade pushing air is a much bigger drag load than a thin disc.

Practical selection method: pick your target tip speed (m/s or mph), pick your weapon diameter, solve for target RPM with the tip-speed formula, then divide by your intended pack voltage (accounting for any gear/belt reduction) to get the KV you need. See formula-required-kv for the exact math and a worked example. If the resulting KV calls for a tiny, high-KV whoop motor to hit a big-diameter target, that is usually a sign to either accept a lower tip speed, add a belt reduction, or move up the size ladder to a lower-KV, higher-torque motor instead of overspeeding a small one.
