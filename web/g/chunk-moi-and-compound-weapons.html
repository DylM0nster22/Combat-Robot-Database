Moment of inertia shapes and building a compound weapon's total MOI

Rotational Fundamentals

antweightplastic-antweightrotationalmoicompound-weaponfundamentals

Moment of inertia (MOI, symbol I, units kg*m^2) is the rotational equivalent of mass: it measures how hard it is to angularly accelerate a spinning object, and it depends heavily on how far the mass sits from the spin axis (radius SQUARED, not just radius). Nearly every weapon-sizing calculation on this page starts from getting I right.

Standard shapes (see the individual formula entities for each): a solid disc/cylinder about its own axis (I = 0.5mr^2), an annulus/washer with a bore (I = 0.5m(r_outer^2+r_inner^2)), a thin ring or thin-walled cylinder where all mass sits at one radius (I = mr^2), a slender rod about its own center as in a horizontal bar spinner (I = (1/12)mL^2), the same rod about one end as in a hammer or axe arm (I = (1/3)mL^2), and a small point mass at a radius as in a counterweight or bolt-on tooth (I = mr^2).

Real weapons are almost never a single pure shape. A typical single-tooth vertical-spinner disc is really a solid disc PLUS a bolted-on hardened tooth sticking out further than the disc's own radius. The parallel axis theorem is what lets you combine these correctly: each part's own moment of inertia about its own center of mass, plus that part's mass times the square of its distance from the ACTUAL rotation axis, summed over every part.

Worked compound example: a 60 g solid disc (radius 28 mm, I_disc = 0.50.0600.028^2 = 2.35e-5 kgm^2, already centered on the rotation axis) plus a 10 g bolt-on tooth, small enough that its own MOI about its own center is negligible, mounted 30 mm from center: tooth contribution = 0 + 0.0100.030^2 = 9.0e-6 kg*m^2. **I_total = 2.35e-5 + 9.0e-6 = 3.25e-5 kg*m^2.**

A useful intuition check: since I scales with r^2, a tooth mounted twice as far out contributes four times as much MOI for the same mass -- which is exactly why single, far-flung teeth are so effective at maximizing stored energy per gram of added weapon mass, at the cost of needing a longer spin-up time for that same reason (more MOI per gram means more torque-seconds needed to reach the same rpm).
