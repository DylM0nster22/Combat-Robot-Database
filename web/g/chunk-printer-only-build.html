Building a plastic ant with only a 3D printer

Fabrication

plastic-antweightprintingplastic-antgetting-started

Plastic antweight exists partly so that a printer plus a box of bought parts is a complete manufacturing capability. A realistic build:

What you print: chassis, wedge or weapon, armour panels, wheels (or wheel hubs with TPU tyres), motor mounts, battery cradle, electronics tray.

What you buy: two drive gearmotors, a weapon motor if you are building one, an integrated receiver/ESC board, a LiPo pack, bearings, M2 and M3 fasteners, heat-set inserts, threadlocker, wire and connectors.

Materials by part:

  • Chassis — PLA+ at 5 perimeters. Stiff, cheap, prints easily, and fails predictably.
  • Armour — TPU 95A, bolted on from outside so it can be swapped in the pits (check your event allows TPU — some plastic-class rules exclude it).
  • Weapon — PETG or PLA+ depending on whether you want it to deform or shatter. Print several.
  • Wheels — TPU tyre on a PLA+ hub, or printed TPU throughout at higher infill.
  • Anywhere a bolt goes — heat-set inserts, never a tapped plastic thread.

Design rules that matter most here:

  1. Orient every part so impacts load layers in compression or shear.
  2. Fillet all internal corners; that is where printed parts crack.
  3. Make armour sacrificial and quick to replace — two bolts, accessible from outside.
  4. Print a complete spare set of every part before the event. This is the class's superpower: your pit repair is a bag of spares, not a machine shop.

The weight reality. A printed chassis is heavier than you expect. Budget it early, weigh printed parts as they come off, and be prepared to reduce infill or perimeters in non-critical areas rather than redesigning at the last minute.

One rules caution. Organisations disagree about post-processing. SPARC's plastic class explicitly bans annealing, acetone smoothing and boiling as durability-enhancing treatments; some other events allow them. Confirm before you rely on any of it.
