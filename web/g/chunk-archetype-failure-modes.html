How each archetype actually fails

Reliability

antweightplastic-antweightreliabilitymaintenance

Archetypes do not fail randomly; each has a characteristic way it dies. Knowing yours tells you what to carry spares for.

Vertical spinners lose the weapon bell off the motor. Stock drone motors retain the bell with a C-clip that was never designed for combat shock loads. They also bend weapon shafts, and a bent shaft means an unbalanced weapon, which destroys bearings within a match.

Bar spinners bend the bar. A bent bar is an unbalanced bar, and the vibration will shake fasteners loose across the whole robot. Carry a spare bar, not a spare bolt.

Drums fail at the hub-to-shaft interface. Set screws back out under vibration and the drum starts slipping on the shaft, which looks like a weapon that spins up slowly and then stops biting.

Horizontal spinners destroy themselves on the arena wall. They also suffer the worst of the self-inflicted-recoil problem — every hit throws them into something.

Wedges and forkbots bend their edges. A curled edge turns a wedge into a ramp, and the robot silently stops working without anything visibly breaking. Check the edge against a flat surface between matches.

Lifters and flippers fail at the pivot or the latch. The arm is a lever that transmits every impact straight into its mounting, and latches that hold spring force are precision parts being hit repeatedly.

Grabbers fail by not grabbing. The mechanism usually survives; the approach does not.

Thwackbots and control bots mostly fail electrically — brownouts on impact, connectors shaking loose, a receiver that resets. With no weapon to break, the failure moves into the wiring.

Everything loses bolts. Thread locker on every fastener, and a torque check between every match, is the highest-value maintenance in the sport.
