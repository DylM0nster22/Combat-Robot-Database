Polycarbonate/Lexan: Strong But Notch-Sensitive

Plastics Deep Dive

antweightplastic-antweightpolycarbonatelexannotch-sensitivityannealingdrilling

Polycarbonate sheet (commonly known by the trade name Lexan) has one of the highest unnotched impact strengths of any common stock plastic — a flat, smooth, unblemished sheet can absorb a very hard blow without cracking, which is exactly why it's a popular window/riot-shield/machine-guard material in the wider world. The catch for combat robot builders is the word "unnotched": polycarbonate's toughness collapses dramatically in the presence of any sharp stress concentrator — a scratch, a nick, a sharp inside corner, or a poorly-drilled hole — because those features locally amplify stress far beyond the nominal load, giving a crack an easy place to start and then propagate straight through material that would otherwise have handled the load fine.

Practical implications for armor design: every hole drilled in a Lexan panel is a potential failure point unless drilled and finished correctly. Best practice is to drill at low speed with a sharp, appropriately-sized bit (never force an undersized hole or use a dull bit that tears rather than cuts), support the sheet firmly to avoid chip-out, and de-burr/chamfer every edge of the hole afterward — a rough or burred hole edge is a stress riser waiting to crack under the next hard hit. The same logic applies to the panel's outer profile: sharp inside corners in a cutout should be radiused rather than left as a sharp right angle, since a sharp corner concentrates stress exactly the way a scratch does.

Annealing: sheet polycarbonate, especially after machining, drilling, or forming (bending), carries residual internal stress from the manufacturing extrusion process plus whatever stress the hobbyist's fabrication added. This residual stress can cause spontaneous stress-cracking over time or under chemical exposure (some solvents/lubricants attack stressed PC) even without an impact. Annealing — a slow bake at roughly 125-130°C for about an hour followed by a slow cool in the still-warm oven — relieves much of this stress and measurably reduces the panel's tendency to crack under both mechanical shock and chemical exposure. Competitive builders who use PC armor generally anneal parts after final machining and before the panel goes into competition.

Other practical notes: PC scratches relatively easily compared to some plastics, and every scratch is a fresh potential crack-initiation site, so handling and storage matter more for PC parts than for a more scratch-tolerant material like UHMW. Standard (non-UV-stabilized) polycarbonate also yellows and embrittles with long UV exposure, which matters for parts stored or displayed outdoors over a long build's lifetime, though this is a minor concern for a part mostly stored indoors between events.
