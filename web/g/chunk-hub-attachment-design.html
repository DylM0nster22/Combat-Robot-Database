Hub attachment design: set screws, keyways, broached hex, and bolt circles

Attachment and Failure

antweightplastic-antweighthubattachmentkeywayhex-boreset-screw

How a weapon actually connects to its shaft determines whether all of that carefully-calculated stored energy stays under control or ends up as a loose, mistimed, or missing weapon mid-match.

Set screws (term-set-screw-hub, component-hub-set-screw) are the lightest and simplest option: one or more screws clamp a round-bore hub onto the shaft by friction, usually biting against a flat ground into the shaft for extra grip. They're cheap, quick to adjust, and adequate for lower-power, lower-shock-load weapons, but are fundamentally a friction joint -- under the shock torque of a hard weapon impact they can slip, which is the term-hub-slip failure mode.

Clamping (split/pinch) hubs (component-hub-clamping) improve on a plain set screw by squeezing the full bore circumference down onto the shaft rather than concentrating force at one point, spreading the grip over more surface area and avoiding denting the shaft the way a point-contact set screw can -- still a friction joint, but a meaningfully more shock-tolerant one.

Keyways (term-keyway) replace friction with a positive mechanical connection: a slot cut into both shaft and hub carries a key that transmits torque directly rather than relying on clamping force. This handles shock loads far better than any set-screw arrangement, at the cost of more precise machining (cutting a clean small keyway in a 4-8 mm ant-scale shaft is fiddly) and a stress concentration at the slot that can itself become the shaft's structural weak point if not sized carefully.

Broached hex bores (term-broached-hex-bore) are the most common positive-drive solution at antweight scale: a hub or pulley with an internal hex hole slides onto a matching hex section of shaft (or hex stock used directly as the shaft), giving keyway-like torque transfer without needing to cut a separate slot and key. It's simple, cheap (hex stock is a standard off-the-shelf item), and handles shock loads well, though it still needs some form of axial retention -- a screw, retaining ring, or shoulder -- since the hex fit alone only prevents rotation, not sliding along the shaft.

Bolt circles. For weapons attached via a ring of bolts rather than a shaft-hub interface (a bolted shell-spinner ring, a bolt-on disc face), the number of fasteners and their radius (the bolt circle) set the joint's torque capacity, quantified in formula-wg-hub-bolt-shear-capacity. In practice, bolted joints at ant scale usually have large margin against pure fastener shear compared to the motor's actual stall torque -- the worked example there shows a 4-screw M2.5 bolt circle capable of roughly 37.7 Nm against a typical weapon motor's 0.05-0.3 Nm stall torque -- which is why real-world hub failures at this scale are overwhelmingly friction-joint slip (set screws) rather than bolts shearing outright.
