Drivetrain sizing: why traction, not torque, usually sets the limit for a 1 lb bot

Drivetrain Fundamentals

antweightplastic-antweightdrivetraintractionfundamentals

Drivetrain sizing for an antweight starts with formula-top-speed-drivetrain: v_top = (rpm_motor/gear_ratio/60) pi D_wheel. A base motor at 11,000 rpm through a 20:1 gearbox and a 32 mm wheel gives an output shaft speed of 550 rpm and a top speed of 0.92 m/s -- solidly in the typical antweight range of 0.6-1.5 m/s.

The trap most new builders fall into is stopping there and assuming more gear reduction (or a beefier motor) automatically means more real-world pushing power. formula-acceleration-from-torque shows the drivetrain COULD deliver a driving force of 20.0 N from a small motor (10 mN*m stall torque) through a 20:1 gearbox at 80% efficiency -- but formula-traction-limited-force shows the wheels can only actually transmit about 3.56 N to the floor before slipping, using a realistic mu = 0.8 and the robot's own 4.453 N weight. Since 20.0 N (available) far exceeds 3.56 N (transmittable), this drivetrain is firmly traction-limited, not torque-limited (formula-wheel-slip-threshold) -- real achievable acceleration is 3.56/0.454 = 7.85 m/s^2, set entirely by wheel grip and robot weight, with huge spare motor torque doing nothing useful.

This has a direct, practical design consequence: for a 454 g robot, spending weight or money on a lower gear ratio or a higher-torque motor rarely improves pushing power once you're already past the point where torque exceeds what the wheels can grip -- which happens at a surprisingly low torque for such a light robot. Better wheel material (higher mu), more weight over the drive wheels, or a wider contact patch improve pushing power far more reliably than more gear reduction.

Climbing an opponent's wedge (formula-torque-to-climb-wedge) requires checking BOTH conditions separately: the torque needed at the wheel to overcome gravity up the slope (usually easily available), and the traction needed to actually grip the (possibly slippery, now-tilted) wedge surface, which caps out entirely at theta_max = atan(mu) regardless of how much torque is on tap -- at mu = 0.8, that's about 38.7 degrees, a hard physical ceiling no amount of extra motor torque can push past.

Power draw stays modest at cruise and only climbs near the traction limit (formula-power-at-speed): P = F*v gives just 0.205 W cruising against rolling resistance, versus 3.28 W pushing at the full traction-limited force while still moving at top speed -- useful bounds when sizing drive ESCs and battery current budget.
