Why PLA/PLA+ Dominates Plastic Antweight Despite Being 'Brittle'

3D-Printed Materials Deep Dive

antweightplastic-antweightplaplastic-antweight-criticaldeep-diveprint-quality

PLA has a reputation, deserved on paper, for being the most brittle of the common FDM filaments — its elongation-to-break is only around 5-8%, far below PETG's ~20%, ABS's ~20%, or nylon's 60-100%+. By that single metric, PLA should be near the bottom of the list for a part expected to survive weapon impacts. In practice, it's the single most-used structural and armor material in plastic-antweight combat robotics, and understanding why reveals more about how the class actually works than the datasheet number does.

Stiffness matters as much as ductility for a single-impact event. A combat robot hit is a very short, very high-force event, not a slow bend test. PLA's high stiffness (elastic modulus around 3.0-3.5 GPa, noticeably higher than PETG's ~2.0-2.2 GPa or ABS's ~1.8-2.2 GPa) means a PLA part resists deforming under load in the first place — for many glancing or moderate hits, the part simply doesn't move enough to reach its (admittedly lower) breaking strain. Ductility only becomes the deciding factor once a hit is hard enough to actually strain the material significantly, and a stiffer part reaches that threshold at a higher force than a more flexible one of the same wall thickness.

Print quality dominates real-world toughness more than material choice. PLA is, by a wide margin, the easiest filament to get consistent, well-adhered layers from on inexpensive, unmodified printers — no enclosure, no heated chamber, minimal warping, forgiving of imperfect cooling and retraction settings. A cracked or delaminated part almost always fails at a layer line or a print defect (a void, poor first-layer adhesion, insufficient wall count) rather than through bulk material fracture, so a material that prints reliably well produces tougher real parts in practice than a "tougher" material printed poorly. Nylon and ABS have better bulk material properties but are meaningfully harder to print defect-free without a proper enclosure and drying setup that many hobbyists don't have.

Cost enables thickness, and thickness is the real armor budget lever. PLA/PLA+ is inexpensive enough (roughly $20-25/kg, same ballpark as PETG/ABS) that builders default to thick walls and moderate-to-high infill (2.5-4mm walls, 40-60%+ infill) rather than thin shells — and because weight-per-area scales with thickness regardless of which plastic is used, the practical comparison in a real build is often "thick PLA" versus "thin nylon" rather than equal-thickness PLA versus nylon, and thick-but-more-brittle frequently outperforms thin-but-tougher in this class.

Disposability is a feature. Because reprinting a cracked PLA shell is cheap and fast (often overnight, for a few dollars of filament), plastic-antweight competitors can treat armor as consumable — expecting to reprint a shell between fights rather than requiring one panel to survive an entire tournament undamaged. This changes the optimization target from "never crack" to "crack in a way that's easy and cheap to replace," which favors PLA's low cost and print ease over a pricier, harder-to-print material that might survive marginally more hits before failing.

PLA+ specifically is a modified PLA blend (formulations vary by brand) that trades a small amount of PLA's stiffness for meaningfully better impact resistance and elongation, narrowing the toughness gap with PETG somewhat while keeping most of standard PLA's easy-printing behavior — many competitive plastic-antweight builders default to PLA+ over standard PLA specifically for this reason when the two cost about the same.
