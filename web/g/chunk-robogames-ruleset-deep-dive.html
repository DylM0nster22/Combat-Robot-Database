RoboGames: Detailed Banned-Weapons List and Point-Scored Judging

Major Rulesets

RoboGames (originally launched as ROBOlympics) is one of the largest general robotics expos in the US, and its combat robotics division is notable for publishing one of the most granular and explicit rulesets in the hobby — both in terms of banned weapons and in terms of exactly how judges score an undecided fight.

RoboGames runs six standard weight classes: 150 g, 1 lb, 3 lb, 60 lb, 120 lb, and 250 lb, plus a full parallel set of walking-bonus classes at exactly double each of those weights (300 g, 2 lb, 6 lb, 120 lb, 240 lb, and 500 lb), and separate 1 lb and 3 lb autonomous-robot divisions for robots that fight without a human driver in the loop.

The banned list is unusually specific, organized loosely around 'invisible damage' weapons (things that hurt a robot's electronics or control system without visible physical damage, which judges and audiences can't easily verify) and 'arena-damaging' weapons (things that would contaminate or damage the fighting surface for the next match): banned items include electrical/RF-jamming weapons, EMF/magnetic fields aimed at disrupting an opponent's electronics, entanglement devices (nets, tape, string), liquid weapons and any weapon that spills liquid, powders, sand, or ball bearings, foams and liquefied gases, untethered projectiles, heat/flame weapons except specially pre-approved flamethrowers, explosives and flammable solids, blinding lights and smoke, and internal combustion engines.

Safety requirements include a manual main power disconnect operable within 15 seconds without endangering the operator and full deactivation achievable within 60 seconds, a weapon lock that must be a clearly visible high-visibility color (neon orange or similar) fitted whenever the robot is powered outside the arena, and a radio system requirement for digitally coded, mated transmitter/receiver pairs with fail-safe — explicitly banning toy-grade radio systems and anything on the AM band.

When a match goes the distance, RoboGames uses a 3-judge panel where each judge independently scores Aggression on a 0-5 point scale and Damage on a 0-6 point scale, for 11 points per judge and 33 points total across the panel. Aggression scoring examples given in the rules: a 5-0 split means one robot never attacked at all, a 4-1 split reflects significant one-sided dominance, and a 3-2 split covers matches where both robots attacked consistently (or, conversely, where both avoided each other about equally) — with credit for aggression specifically requiring 'translational movement toward the opponent,' not just spinning a weapon in place. Damage is bucketed into six severity tiers (trivial, cosmetic, minor, significant, major, massive) before being converted to a judge's point total. Pinning an opponent is capped at 15 seconds before the pinning robot must back off enough to let the opponent attempt to escape.
