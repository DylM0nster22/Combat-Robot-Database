Storage, Disposal, and Shipping/Flying with LiPos

Safety

antweightplastic-antweightstoragedisposalshippingair-travelsafety

Long-term storage: any pack not going to be used within the next few days should be brought to storage voltage, roughly 3.8V/cell (acceptable range about 3.7-3.85V/cell). This is the voltage at which the cell's internal chemistry sits in its most stable, lowest-stress state. Store packs in a LiPo-safe bag or a fireproof container, away from direct sunlight, extreme heat, or anything flammable, and check on packs stored for a long time periodically (over months, voltage will slowly drift and should be topped back up if it falls too low).

Disposal: a pack at true end of life (persistently puffed, damaged, or no longer holding a usable charge) should be fully discharged before disposal — many hobby shops and some municipal hazardous-waste programs accept LiPo packs for proper recycling/disposal; check local rules rather than simply throwing a pack in household trash, since a damaged cell can still short and ignite in a garbage truck or landfill. A genuinely swollen or physically damaged pack should not be "one more cycle"-tested to use up its capacity before disposal — isolate and dispose of it as-is rather than experimenting with a compromised cell.

Shipping LiPos by mail or courier is regulated (LiPo/lithium batteries are classified as hazardous material for transport in most jurisdictions) — small antweight-size packs are usually well under the watt-hour thresholds that trigger the strictest hazmat shipping rules, but sellers and shippers still generally require packs to be shipped at a partial state of charge (often at or near storage voltage) and properly packaged (terminals covered/isolated, cushioned, sometimes in a dedicated LiPo-safe shipping bag) to limit short-circuit and fire risk in transit.

Flying with LiPos (air travel to an event): airlines and aviation regulators (in the US, TSA/FAA rules) generally require spare lithium batteries — which most competitors' antweight packs are, since they travel disconnected from the robot — to go in carry-on baggage only, never checked luggage, specifically because a battery fire in the cargo hold is far harder to detect and fight than one in the cabin. Batteries typically must have terminals protected from short-circuiting (tape over exposed connector pins, or individual plastic bags/battery cases) and are usually subject to a per-battery watt-hour limit for carry-on without special airline approval (commonly cited around 100 Wh per battery for unrestricted carry-on, with a smaller number of higher-capacity batteries allowed with airline permission above that) — small antweight packs are so far under this threshold (a few Wh each, see formula-pack-energy-wh) that the practical requirement for most antweight travelers is simply: pack them in carry-on, in a LiPo-safe bag, with terminals covered, and be ready to show them if asked at security.
