What a Technical/Safety Inspector Actually Checks

Safety & Inspection

Every combat robot, from a $30 plastic antweight to a $50,000 BattleBots heavyweight, passes through a technical and safety inspection before it is allowed to compete, and while the depth of inspection scales enormously with weight class, the checklist items are drawn from the same underlying list everywhere. For an antweight or plastic-antweight builder showing up to their first event, a realistic run-through of what an inspector checks looks roughly like this, in a typical order:

  1. Weigh-in: the robot, fully assembled and ready to fight (batteries installed, all fasteners tight, nothing missing), is placed on a scale and must be at or under the class limit (e.g. 454 g for US antweight, 150 g for UK antweight/fairyweight, or the appropriate walker-bonus weight if claiming that allowance). Many inspectors also re-verify weight after any mid-event repair.
  2. Dimension/footprint check: some rulesets (notably AWS/UK) also require the robot fit within a defined footprint (150mm x 150mm square for UK antweight-scale classes) with no overhang at any height, checked with a simple frame or template.
  3. Power disconnect check: the inspector locates and tests the main power switch or removable link, confirming it is externally accessible, operable without tools, and actually cuts all power when used.
  4. Weapon lock check: for any robot with an active weapon, the inspector confirms a proper high-visibility lock is fitted and prevents the weapon from moving while power is on, and that it's easy for pit staff to see at a glance whether the robot is 'safe' or 'live.'
  5. Radio fail-safe test: the inspector or driver arms the robot, then deliberately turns off or unplugs the transmitter while watching the robot; a pass means all motion (drive and weapon) stops immediately, and repeats may be requested to rule out a fluke.
  6. Battery/wiring check: batteries are inspected for physical damage, swelling, or exposed wiring; connectors are checked for secure fit and correct polarity; any battery pack that looks damaged or that exceeds the class's voltage cap (e.g. AWS's 24V limit) is rejected outright.
  7. Banned materials/weapons check: the inspector visually confirms no banned weapon types are present (entanglement devices, liquid reservoirs, explosive/flame elements, RF-jamming hardware) and, for plastic-class entries, may spot-check that structural parts are visibly plastic and not a banned filament like TPU, nylon, or a carbon-fiber composite passed off as standard PLA/ABS.
  8. Sharp edge / pinch point check: at even the smallest scale, inspectors look for exposed sharp edges, unshielded gears, or pinch points that could injure a handler during ordinary pit handling, independent of the robot's in-arena weapon.
  9. Sign-off: once everything passes, the robot is tagged/logged as cleared to fight, sometimes with a sticker or wristband system tying the robot to its driver and confirmed weight class.

For a plastic antweight in particular, inspection is usually fast (often under two minutes) precisely because the class's material and weight limits are tight, weapon energy is low, and there's little to check beyond the basics above — one more reason it remains the easiest class to show up to an event with for the first time and get through check-in without surprises.
