Drum spinner geometry: diameter, wall thickness, and tooth design

Weapon Forms

antweightplastic-antweightdrum-spinnergeometrydead-shaft

A drum spinner rotates about a horizontal axis running across the width of the robot (unlike a disc or bar, which typically spin on an axis running front-to-back or side-to-side relative to the direction of travel), striking with teeth or fangs set into a cylindrical (or egg-shaped) body that spans most of the bot's width.

Diameter and wall thickness. Drum diameter is often constrained by the robot's ground clearance and wheel size as much as by weapon design intent, since the drum usually sits between or in front of the drive wheels. For a fixed outer diameter and total mass, wall thickness controls MOI the same way rim-loading does on a disc: a thin-walled drum with mass concentrated near the outer shell carries meaningfully more stored energy than a thick-walled drum of the same weight and diameter (formula-wg-drum-wall-thickness-moi shows a ~38% MOI difference between a 3 mm and a 10 mm wall at the same 40 mm OD and mass). In practice this pushes competitive antweight drums toward a hardened, relatively thin outer shell reinforced by a lighter internal spine or core rather than a solid steel cylinder.

Tooth/fang design. Teeth are usually separate hardened inserts (bolted, pinned, or welded) set into pockets machined into the drum body, following the same insert-vs-monolithic tradeoff as disc teeth (term-hardened-insert). Beveling the tooth so the leading face presents a sharper, more acute edge to the opponent -- rather than a flat/blunt face -- generally improves cutting/biting performance for the same tooth material and hardness. Drum tooth counts and spacing follow the same bite-depth-vs-hit-frequency tradeoff as disc teeth (see the bite section), but drums typically run more teeth around the circumference than a disc of similar diameter because a drum's whole width is engaging the opponent per pass, not just a point on the rim.

Egg-shaped vs cylindrical. Most antweight drums are true cylinders for simplicity of balancing and manufacturing. An egg-shaped (eccentric) drum (term-egg-shaped-drum) varies the effective striking radius through the rotation for a mixed sweep-then-strike effect, but is substantially harder to balance and is a comparatively rare, advanced choice at antweight scale.

Dead-shaft construction. Antweight drums are very commonly built dead-shaft (term-dead-shaft): the shaft bolts rigidly to the frame, and the drum spins around it on bearings pressed into its own end caps or hub, driven by a pulley/sprocket bonded or bolted to the drum body (a 'bell'). This keeps the bearings inside the drum near its mass rather than needing a rotating shaft supported far out at the frame rails, but makes bell retention (see term-bell-separation) a key failure point to design against.
