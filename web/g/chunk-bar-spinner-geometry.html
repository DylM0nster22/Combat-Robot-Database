Bar spinner geometry: length, cross-section, tip mass, and canting

Weapon Forms

antweightplastic-antweightbar-spinnergeometrymoi

A bar spinner replaces the disc's flat plane with a long, narrow bar rotating about its center (or, on some builds, off-center) on a vertical or horizontal axis. The defining geometric choices are overall length, cross-section shape, how mass is distributed along the bar, and whether it's built symmetric or asymmetric.

Length and tip mass. Bar length sets the tip radius directly, and because MOI for a slender bar scales with length squared (formula-moi-slender-rod-center in the design-math topic), a longer bar dramatically increases both stored energy and required spin-up torque for the same total mass. Antweight bars commonly run 60-90 mm tip-to-tip. Concentrating extra mass at the tips (a wider paddle, a bolted-on tip weight, or a hardened tip insert) further increases MOI beyond a uniform bar of the same total weight, similar in spirit to the disc's rim-loading effect, because tip mass sits at the point of maximum radius.

Worked example. A 70 mm long, 60 g bar spinning about its center (35 mm to each tip): treating it as a uniform slender rod, I = (1/12)mL^2 = (1/12)0.0600.070^2 = 2.45e-5 kgm^2. At 18,000 rpm (omega = 1885 rad/s), tip speed v = omegar = 18850.035 = 66.0 m/s (about 148 mph), and stored energy KE = 0.5Iomega^2 = 0.52.45e-5*1885^2 = 43.5 J -- solidly in the hard-hitting range for a 1 lb spinner.

Symmetric vs asymmetric. Bar spinners must be mass-balanced end to end (equal effective mass times radius on each side of the shaft) but do not have to look symmetric -- a common antweight pattern is a single hardened striking tip on one end balanced by a shorter, denser counterweight mass on the other, which concentrates more of the total weight budget into the one edge that actually does the cutting, at the cost of a slightly trickier balance job than a mirror-image bar.

Cross-section. Bars are typically rectangular or a flattened oval in cross-section rather than round, giving a wide flat striking face while keeping material close to the rotation plane; the cross-section also needs enough section depth (see formula-wg-cantilever-shaft-bending for the general bending-stress relationship) to resist bending/whipping under impact, particularly near the center hub where the bending moment from an off-center hit is highest.

Canted bars. See term-canted-bar for bars deliberately angled out of a single plane to impart a lifting/deflecting component to hits or to reduce direct lockups with an opposing spinner, traded against a less mechanically efficient direct hit and a harder balance job.
