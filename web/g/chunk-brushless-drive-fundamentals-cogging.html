Brushless Drive for Antweights: 1104/1106/1404 Motors, Pods, and Low-Speed Cogging

Drive Motors

Brushless drive on antweights has grown mainly out of the FPV micro-drone motor market. Motors labeled by a four-digit size code — 1104, 1106, 1404, and similar — describe their stator dimensions (the first two digits are diameter in mm, the last two are stack height/length in mm): an '1104' is roughly 11mm in diameter and 4mm tall, an '1106' is the same 11mm diameter but a taller 6mm stack (more copper/stator volume, meaning more achievable torque and current handling at similar RPM), and a '1404' steps up to a 14mm diameter for still more capability in a similarly slim package. These motors are sold at a range of kv (RPM per volt) windings, and combat builders — or companies like Repeat Robotics who now sell them pre-integrated — pick a kv and pair it with a small gear reduction (commonly a 2-3 stage planetary gearbox in the 16-24mm class) to land in a useful antweight speed/torque range.

The major practical difference from brushed drive is that brushless motors require a reversing brushless ESC rather than a simple brushed H-bridge, and most of these motors are sensorless — they infer rotor position from back-EMF rather than dedicated Hall-effect sensors. Back-EMF is proportional to rotor speed, so at very low RPM (exactly the speed range a combat robot spends a lot of time in — creeping, jockeying for position, or making a fine positioning move) the signal an ESC needs to commutate smoothly gets weak and noisy. This shows up as cogging: jerky, notchy, sometimes stalling rotation right around zero speed, instead of the smooth linear response a brushed motor gives down to a full stop. Sensored brushless motors (with embedded Hall sensors) avoid this because the ESC always has a clean absolute rotor-position signal regardless of speed, but sensored micro combat motors are rarer and typically cost more; the more common combat-robotics fix is a gear reduction high enough that the motor itself is still spinning at a meaningfully high electrical RPM even when the wheel is barely turning, combined with an ESC/firmware tuned specifically for low-speed sensorless combat driving (as opposed to a generic multirotor ESC optimized for one direction and a fast spin-up) — which is exactly what purpose-built combat brushless ESCs like Repeat Robotics' reversing ESC are designed to handle.

The upside that keeps builders choosing brushless despite this complexity is power density and efficiency: no brushes to wear out, generally more torque and speed available per gram of motor than an equivalent brushed can, and (since kv and voltage set speed directly) the ability to retune top speed just by changing battery cell count (2S/3S/4S) rather than swapping hardware, useful for quickly adapting a drivetrain between a slower control-bot setup and a faster spinner-chassis setup.
