Worked Example: The Traction Limit and Why Extra Torque Gets Wasted

Formulas

No matter how much torque a drivetrain can theoretically produce, it can only push as hard as the wheels' grip on the floor allows before they simply spin in place. That ceiling is the traction limit: F_max = μ x N, where μ is the coefficient of friction between the wheel tread and the arena floor, and N is the normal force (weight) pressing the driven wheels down.

Worked example: A 1 lb robot weighs 0.4536 kg x 9.81 m/s² = 4.45 N. Fit it with silicone-tread wheels giving a friction coefficient around μ = 0.6 against a typical polycarbonate arena floor (foam alone would be lower, roughly 0.3-0.5; a soft compliant tread can push toward 0.7-0.8). F_max = 0.6 x 4.45 = 2.67 N — that's the absolute maximum forward force this robot can generate on this floor, no matter what drivetrain sits behind the wheels.

Now compare that to what the drivetrain itself can supply. Take the lowest-torque stock Silver Spark ratio, 11.1:1, with an estimated output stall torque around 0.38 kg-cm (≈0.0373 N·m) per motor, on the same 1.5in-diameter (0.019m radius) wheels, split across 2 driven wheels: the wheels together can theoretically deliver up to F = 2 x T / r = 2 x 0.0373 / 0.019 ≈ 3.9 N of force before the motors stall out. That's already above the 2.67 N traction ceiling — meaning this robot's wheels will slip and spin before its motors ever reach their torque limit, even at the fastest, lowest-torque ratio in the whole Silver Spark lineup.

This is the core reason "just gear it down more for more pushing power" is often a wasted move on a well-traction-matched antweight: once a drivetrain's available torque already exceeds the traction limit, adding more torque (by choosing a higher, slower gear ratio) doesn't increase real-world pushing force at all — the extra torque just goes into wheel slip, heat, and battery drain, while the robot gets slower and less maneuverable for no pushing benefit. The useful design target, as commonly recommended in the antweight community, is to size the drivetrain with roughly 1.5-2x the torque strictly required to reach the traction limit (giving margin for wear, voltage sag, and imperfect weight distribution) and then spend any additional gear-ratio choice on speed and control feel rather than further torque, since torque beyond that point is traction-limited, not motor-limited.
