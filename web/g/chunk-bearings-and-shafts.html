Bearings, shafts and why weapons come apart

Assembly

antweightplastic-antweightfabricationweapon-designreliability

The weapon mounting is where most antweight mechanical failures happen. Getting it right is mostly about stiffness and retention.

Shaft diameter dominates. Bending stiffness scales with the fourth power of diameter. Going from a 3 mm shaft to 5 mm is roughly 7.7 times stiffer for about 2.8 times the mass. On any weapon storing real energy that is among the best weight-for-reliability trades available. A bent shaft is not just a bent shaft — it is an unbalanced weapon, which then destroys its own bearings within a match.

Support both ends where you can. A cantilevered weapon on a single bearing puts a huge bending moment into that one point. A dead-shaft arrangement — fixed shaft, weapon spinning on bearings around it, supported on both sides — is far stronger and is the standard approach for drums.

Bearing choice. Shielded (ZZ) deep-groove ball bearings in standard metric sizes: 623 for 3 mm shafts, 625 for 5 mm, 688 for 8 mm dead shafts. Buy them as consumables. A bearing that takes a hard impact gets brinelled — the balls dent the races — and runs rough from then on.

Consider bronze bushings. They have more friction than ball bearings but nothing inside to dent, so they tolerate impact far better. On a weapon with torque to spare, a bushing can outlast several bearings.

Retention is the failure everyone hits. The classic antweight failure is the weapon coming off the motor. Stock drone motors retain the bell with a C-clip never intended for combat shock loads. Hubs held by a single grub screw on a round shaft will slip and then spin freely. The fixes, in order of effectiveness: a machined flat on the shaft for the grub screw to bear against; two grub screws at 90 degrees; a broached hex or a keyway; threadlocker on the grub screws regardless.

Press fits. A bearing seat wants a light interference — the bearing goes in with hand pressure or a light tap. In printed parts, expect to tune the hole diameter for your specific printer. A bearing that spins in its seat destroys the seat.
