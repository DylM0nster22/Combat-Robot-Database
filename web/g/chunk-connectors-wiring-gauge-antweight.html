Connectors and Wiring for Antweight Battery Systems

Wiring

antweightplastic-antweightconnectorswiringxt30jst

Connector choice on an antweight battery system is a direct current-handling decision, not just a matter of what the pack ships with. JST-PH (2.0mm pitch) connectors are standard on the smallest 1S-2S micro packs and are used essentially everywhere as the balance-lead connector regardless of pack size, but they are only rated for a few amps continuous — fine as a main-power connector on the very smallest, lowest-draw antweight builds, but a real bottleneck (and a source of resistive heating that worsens voltage sag) once combined drive+weapon draw climbs into double-digit amps. XT30 is the common step-up connector, rated for roughly 15-30A depending on source and wire gauge, and is what most of the combat-market-specific and combat-crossover packs in the 300-550mAh antweight range (CNHL MiniStar, GNB LiHV 4S) ship with as standard. Larger XT60/XT90 connectors exist but are oversized and unnecessarily heavy for realistic antweight current levels; they belong in beetleweight and up.

Wire gauge matters as much as the connector: a pack's connector rating is only as good as the wire feeding it, and the thin factory leads on some micro packs (commonly 20-26AWG on the smallest packs) can become the actual current bottleneck and heat source even when the connector itself is rated higher. Builders running a demanding weapon setup sometimes re-terminate a pack's factory JST lead to XT30 and upgrade to a slightly heavier gauge wire in the same operation, both to raise the safe current ceiling and to reduce the resistive voltage drop (and associated heat) in the wiring itself — effectively reducing the system's total internal-resistance-like losses on top of whatever the cells themselves contribute, which directly helps with voltage sag under weapon spin-up.

A secondary but important wiring practice is keeping the battery's main power leads short and direct to the ESCs/power distribution point; every extra centimeter of thin wire between pack and load adds resistance and therefore adds to voltage drop under load, compounding the sag problem discussed elsewhere in this file. In a chassis as small as an antweight's, this is rarely a big factor in absolute terms, but it costs nothing to route the main power leads as short and direct as the layout allows rather than coiling excess length inside the electronics bay.
