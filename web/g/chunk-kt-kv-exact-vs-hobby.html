Kt from Kv: the exact relation vs the hobby shorthand, and why the difference matters

Electrical Fundamentals

antweightplastic-antweightelectricalktkvmotor-constantfundamentals

Every brushed or brushless DC motor has two closely related constants: Kv (rpm produced per volt of back-EMF, at no load) and Kt (torque produced per amp of current). They describe the SAME underlying physical motor constant, just in different units, and are related by a fixed physical law -- but the combat-robotics hobby commonly uses a shortcut formula that is NOT the same thing.

The exact, physically derived relation is: **Kt [Nm/A] = 60 / (2pi*Kv[rpm/V]) = 9.5493 / Kv**. This comes directly from the definitions of Kv and Kt in consistent SI units and holds for an ideal motor regardless of size, winding, or efficiency.

The hobby shorthand commonly seen in RC and combat robotics circles is Kt = 8.27 / Kv. This is NOT a different physical law -- it is the exact relation with an assumed real-world mechanical/electrical efficiency of roughly 87% already multiplied in (8.27/9.5493 = 0.866). It's a convenient "expected real output torque" estimate for a typical small hobby motor, but it silently bundles in a loss assumption that may not match your actual motor.

Why this matters: if you use the exact 9.5493/Kv form to compute Kt, then SEPARATELY apply your own efficiency estimate downstream (e.g. in formula-motor-efficiency-estimate), your math is internally consistent. But if you use the hobby's 8.27/Kv shorthand AND THEN also apply a separate efficiency derating on top of it, you are double-counting losses -- understating real available torque by roughly 13-25%+ depending on the second derating applied. This directly skews spin-up time predictions (formula-spin-up-time) and stall-torque estimates (formula-stall-torque) toward being overly pessimistic (or, if done backwards, overly optimistic).

Worked comparison for a Kv = 2750 rpm/V motor: Kt_exact = 9.5493/2750 = 0.003472 Nm/A. Kt_hobby = 8.27/2750 = 0.003007 Nm/A -- 86.6% of the exact value. Every other worked example on this page uses the exact 9.5493/Kv form for Kt, and applies efficiency as a separate, explicit factor where relevant, so the two effects can always be told apart.
