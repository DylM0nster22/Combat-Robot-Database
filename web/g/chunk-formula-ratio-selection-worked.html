Worked Example: Reverse-Solving Gear Ratio from a Target Top Speed

Formulas

When a builder starts from a target top speed instead of a specific motor, the speed formula can be rearranged to solve directly for the gear ratio needed: gear_ratio = motor_rpm_no_load x pi x wheel_diameter(in) / (target_mph x 1056).

Worked example: Suppose the goal is an 8 mph top speed, using a FingerTech Silver Spark (motor_rpm_no_load = 11,530 at 7.4V) and a 2.0in wheel.

gear_ratio = 11,530 x 3.1416 x 2.0 / (8 x 1056) = 72,444 / 8,448 ≈ 8.6:1

But Silver Spark's fastest stock ratio is 11.1:1, not 8.6:1 — there's no off-the-shelf ratio fast enough to hit exactly 8 mph with this motor and this wheel size. This is a completely normal outcome of the reverse calculation, and it points to two practical fixes:

  1. Increase the wheel diameter and re-check the fastest available stock ratio. At 11.1:1 with a larger 2.5in wheel: wheel RPM = 11,530 / 11.1 = 1,038.7, so v(mph) = 1,038.7 x 3.1416 x 2.5 / 1056 ≈ 7.7 mph — very close to the 8 mph target, achieved with a completely off-the-shelf Silver Spark by simply choosing a bigger wheel instead of hunting for a nonexistent ratio.
  2. Switch to a higher-RPM drivetrain entirely, most commonly a brushless drive pod. Because a brushless motor's speed is set by kv x voltage rather than a fixed brush-motor RPM, a brushless antweight gearmotor can be tuned well past this target simply by running a higher-cell-count battery (3S or 4S instead of 2S), without touching the gearbox or wheel at all.

The general lesson from reverse-solving gear ratio this way is that wheel diameter and gear ratio are interchangeable knobs for hitting a speed target — a builder isn't locked into whatever stock ratios a motor happens to ship in, because changing wheel size (within the traction and torque limits from the other formulas) shifts the achievable speed just as effectively as changing the gearbox. The final choice usually comes down to whichever knob has the least negative side effect on the rest of the build: a bigger wheel raises ground clearance and can increase rotational inertia (slower spin-up/braking) and often adds weight, while a different gear ratio may not exist as a stock part, and a different motor altogether means a bigger BOM and design change.
