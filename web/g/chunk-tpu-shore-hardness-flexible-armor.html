TPU Shore Hardness: The Most Important Plastic-Antweight Material

3D-Printed Materials Deep Dive

antweightplastic-antweighttpushore-hardnessflexible-armordeep-diveplastic-antweight-critical

If a single material development explains the current state of the art in plastic-antweight armor, it's flexible TPU (thermoplastic polyurethane) printed at a tuned Shore hardness. Shore hardness measures resistance to indentation on a defined scale — Shore A for softer, more rubber-like materials and Shore D for firmer, more plastic-like materials — and the number alone tells a builder roughly how the material will behave under a weapon impact before they ever print a test piece.

95A — the combat-robot default: at 95A, TPU sits near the firm end of the Shore A range (rubber bands are roughly 20-40A; a shopping cart wheel is roughly 90-100A), stiff enough to hold a usable shape and print reasonably well, but still hugely more elastic than any rigid plastic — typical elongation-to-break exceeds 400-500%. This combination makes 95A the community's most common choice for a flexible outer armor skin: it deforms substantially under a spinner or hammer impact, spreading the force over a larger contact patch and a longer time window than a rigid material would, then relaxes back toward its original shape once the load is removed. Because the deformation is largely elastic rather than the plastic (permanent) deformation UHMW undergoes, a 95A panel can typically take many separate hits without visibly accumulating damage the way a rigid shell accumulates cracks.

85A — softer, more absorbing, harder to print: dropping to 85A gives noticeably more compliance (it compresses more readily, closer to a gel-like feel versus 95A's firmer-rubber feel), which can improve impact absorption further, especially against sharper or more penetrating weapons. The tradeoff is print reliability: softer filament is harder to push through a Bowden-style extruder (the tube can let the filament buckle/kink under feed pressure before it reaches the hot end), so 85A generally requires a direct-drive extruder and slower print speeds to print reliably, which is part of why 95A remains more popular among builders without direct-drive printers.

60D — the semi-rigid bridge: measured instead on the Shore D scale (roughly comparable to a very firm 95-98A), 60D TPU behaves much more like a stiff rubber than a soft one — it holds structural shape far better than 85A/95A while still flexing rather than cracking under a sharp glancing hit. This makes it useful for parts that need to do double duty: wedge skirts or hinge components that see real structural/mounting loads in addition to occasional impacts, where full 85A/95A softness would be too compliant to be useful structurally.

Why this matters more than any single rigid material: rigid plastics (PLA, PETG, ABS, nylon) all eventually face the same failure mode under a hard enough hit — they crack, because their strategy is to resist deformation up to a breaking point. TPU sidesteps that failure mode entirely by not resisting deformation in the first place, at the cost of contributing essentially zero structural rigidity on its own. The resulting design pattern — a rigid PLA or PETG frame doing the structural/mounting work, wrapped or skinned in TPU 95A (or 85A at the highest-risk points) doing the impact-absorbing work — is why TPU is arguably the single most important material a plastic-antweight builder can add to their toolkit beyond the base structural filament.
