Designing a Weapon That Must Be Plastic: Blunt Force Over Cutting

Weapon Design

plastic-antweightweapon-designprintingdrumsdiscs

Every plastic-ant weapon designer faces the same core constraint: no metal weapon teeth, no hardened cutting edge, no dense tungsten or steel mass concentrated at the tip. The entire striking body of the weapon has to be one of the approved printable plastics. This has pushed the class toward a distinct design doctrine: blunt force over cutting.

Printed drums are the clearest expression of this doctrine — rather than a thin blade, the weapon is a full cylindrical wall, often 15-25mm thick in PETG, sometimes with small printed paddles or teeth around its circumference. The wall thickness itself (not a sharpened edge) delivers the hit, more like being struck by a rolling pin than cut by a knife. Damage to an opponent comes from blunt impact transferring kinetic energy into denting their armor or knocking them off-balance/into the arena wall, not from penetration.

Printed discs for vertical spinners follow the same logic at a smaller scale: a thick disc (often reinforced with a metal hub insert bonded or press-fit into the printed body) with a rounded or lightly toothed rim outperforms a disc with sharp printed teeth, because printed teeth are exactly the thin, stress-concentrated geometry most likely to snap off on first contact.

What actually does damage without metal teeth: mass times velocity squared delivered over a wide contact patch. A heavy, slow-spinning printed drum that reliably survives its own hits and knocks an opponent around consistently outperforms a fast, light printed blade that shatters on its first real impact. Community guides repeatedly emphasize over-building the weapon's wall thickness and using more print perimeters/walls than seems necessary, accepting the weight cost, because a weapon that survives to keep hitting is worth more than a lighter one that self-destructs in the first exchange.

Practical design rules that have emerged:

  • Keep the weapon's outer profile as a smooth curve or wide flat face rather than a pointed tooth wherever possible.
  • Orient the print so that the load path runs along layer lines, not across them, at the weapon's highest-stress point (often means printing a disc/drum flat rather than on its edge).
  • Use a metal shaft/hub insert (explicitly rules-legal as an axle exemption) so the plastic body isn't also trying to survive the torsional load of driving the weapon, only the impact load of hitting something.
  • Print weapons in PETG or well-annealed ABS over plain PLA/PLA+ wherever the weapon is expected to take real hits, since both have more ductile (bend-before-break) failure behavior.
