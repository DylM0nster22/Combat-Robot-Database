Worked Example: Sizing Motor Torque for a Target Acceleration

Formulas

Once a target top speed sets the gear ratio in the ballpark, the next question is whether that ratio can actually deliver the acceleration or pushing force the robot needs — this is a torque-sizing problem, not a speed problem.

Start from Newton's second law, F = m x a, to find the total force the robot needs at the ground. Convert that to a torque requirement at the wheel by multiplying by wheel radius and dividing across however many wheels share the load: T_wheel = F x r / n_driven_wheels. Finally, work back through the gearbox to the motor shaft itself: T_motor_required = T_wheel / (gear_ratio x efficiency), using roughly 0.65-0.80 efficiency for a typical spur gearbox (like Silver Spark) or 0.80-0.92 for a planetary gearbox (like Repeat Robotics' motors).

Worked example: Take a standard 1 lb robot, m = 0.4536 kg, targeting an aggressive 2g acceleration (a = 19.6 m/s²) — a reasonable target for a bot that wants to close distance quickly or absorb a ram — on two driven wheels of 1.5in diameter (radius r = 0.019 m).

  1. F = m x a = 0.4536 x 19.6 = 8.89 N
  2. T_wheel (per side) = F x r / n = 8.89 x 0.019 / 2 = 0.0844 N·m ≈ 0.86 kg-cm
  3. At a 33.3:1 gear ratio with ~0.75 gearbox efficiency: T_motor_required = 0.86 / (33.3 x 0.75) ≈ 0.0345 kg-cm

Compare that 0.0345 kg-cm requirement to the Silver Spark's bare-motor stall torque of roughly 0.046 kg-cm: there's real margin, meaning 33.3:1 comfortably supports a 2g start. Repeat the same math at the fastest stock ratio, 11.1:1: T_motor_required = 0.86 / (11.1 x 0.75) ≈ 0.103 kg-cm — more than double the bare motor's stall torque, meaning 11.1:1 alone cannot sustain a full 2g acceleration all the way from a dead stop under stall conditions (it can still accelerate, just not at that aggressive a rate, and the motor will spend less time near stall as RPM builds).

The practical takeaway: faster (lower) gear ratios trade away low-speed acceleration/pushing headroom for top speed, and slower (higher) ratios do the reverse. A builder picks the ratio that keeps enough torque margin for the robot's actual job (a control bot needs more low-end torque margin than a spinner that mostly just needs to reposition) — but as the next formula shows, that torque margin only matters up to the point where the wheels can actually put it to the ground.
