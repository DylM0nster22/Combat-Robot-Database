Motor constants: Kt, back-EMF, resistance, and estimating stall torque & peak power

KV & Motor Constants

antweightplastic-antweightmotor-constantstorquethermal

Beyond KV, three more numbers let you actually estimate how a weapon motor will perform rather than just guess from its size class.

Torque constant, Kt. For a sinusoidally-driven brushless motor, Kt (Nm/A) = 8.27 / Kv (RPM/V). This falls straight out of the physics linking electrical and mechanical power in an ideal motor (the 8.27 factor comes from unit conversion between RPM and rad/s and the specific definitions used for Kv and Kt). A 2207 at 1950kv has Kt = 8.27/1950 = 0.00424 Nm/A -- meaning every amp of current produces about 4.24 mN*m of torque. A lower-KV motor of the same stator, say 1300kv, would have Kt = 8.27/1300 = 0.00636 Nm/A, producing 50% more torque per amp, at the cost of proportionally lower RPM for the same voltage.

Back-EMF and the torque-speed line. As the motor spins, it generates a back-EMF voltage proportional to RPM/Kv that opposes the applied drive voltage. At stall (RPM=0) back-EMF is zero, so current is limited only by winding resistance: I_stall = V_applied / R_winding. As RPM rises toward the no-load speed (RPM = Kv * V_applied), back-EMF rises to nearly cancel the applied voltage, current drops toward zero, and torque (which is proportional to current via Kt) drops toward zero along a straight line from stall torque down to zero at no-load speed. This straight-line torque-speed relationship is why average torque during spin-up is commonly approximated as about half of stall torque.

Winding resistance and copper loss. Resistance (ohms, phase-to-phase) sets both stall current and how much of your electrical power is wasted as heat rather than converted to torque: P_copper_loss = I^2 * R. Weapon-motor stalls -- hitting a wall, jamming against an opponent's weapon, an opponent pinning your spinner -- push current toward I_stall = V/R, which for a typical 22xx-class weapon motor (R around 0.05-0.09 ohm) on a 4S pack (16.8V) can mean 180-330A of instantaneous stall current if nothing else limited it. In practice, ESC current limiting, battery internal resistance, and the wiring/connectors all clamp this well below the theoretical stall current, but repeated hard stalls still dump serious heat into the stator in a very short time -- this is the direct mechanism behind burnt windings.

Estimating stall torque and peak power. Stall torque = Kt I_stall_actual (using your realistic, current-limited stall current, not the theoretical V/R number). Peak mechanical power occurs at half the no-load speed and half of stall torque, i.e. P_peak = 0.25 tau_stall * omega_noload -- a useful ballpark for how much mechanical power a given motor/battery/ESC combination can actually deliver to the weapon during the most demanding part of spin-up.
