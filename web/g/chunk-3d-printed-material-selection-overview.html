3D-Printed Material Selection for Plastic Antweights: Overview

3D-Printed Materials Deep Dive

antweightplastic-antweight3d-printedoverviewplastic-antweightfilament-selection

Plastic-antweight is defined by its reliance on hobby FDM 3D printing for structure and armor, which makes filament selection the single biggest set of material decisions in the class. Here's how the common options stack up against each other for a builder deciding what to load into the printer next.

PLA/PLA+ remains the default starting point and, for many competitive builders, the default finishing point too — see the dedicated PLA dominance discussion for the full reasoning, but in short: cheapest, easiest to print reliably, stiffest of the common options, and cheap enough to treat as disposable/reprintable between fights.

PETG is the most common upgrade path when a builder wants more ductility/toughness than PLA without taking on ABS's warping problems or nylon's moisture sensitivity — it's the "more forgiving toughness" option, at some cost in stiffness and print cleanliness (stringing).

ABS/ASA earns its place mainly through heat resistance (useful for parts mounted near a hot weapon motor or ESC) and, for ASA specifically, UV/weather resistance — not generally chosen for extra impact toughness over PETG, and it demands more printer setup (heated bed, ideally an enclosure) to avoid warping and weak inter-layer bonds.

Nylon (PA6/PA12) offers the best all-round bulk toughness and fatigue resistance of the common printables — excellent for living hinges, gears, and high-flex mounts — but its strong hygroscopicity (it absorbs moisture from ambient air within hours and prints poorly wet) and warping tendency make it less common than PLA/PETG for full armor shells; it shows up more in mechanisms than outer panels. PA-CF (carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon) pushes stiffness and dry strength even higher for structural, non-impact-facing parts, at the cost of added brittleness and serious nozzle wear.

PC filament offers the highest strength/heat-resistance ceiling of common printables but requires high-temperature, typically enclosed printing setups that exclude a lot of hobby-grade machines, so it's a premium/advanced option rather than a mainstream choice.

PP (polypropylene) is the lightest common filament and has outstanding fatigue resistance for living-hinge features, but its notoriously poor bed adhesion makes it hard to print reliably, restricting it to small flexible features (snap latches, hinges) rather than structural or armor use.

TPU (95A/85A/60D) is the outlier on this list because it isn't rigid at all — it's the compliant, energy-absorbing skin material discussed in depth elsewhere, used over a rigid frame from one of the materials above rather than as a replacement for one.

Carbon/glass-filled variants of PLA, PETG, or nylon trade toughness for stiffness and are best reserved for structural members set back from direct impact (frame rails, weapon-motor mount arms) rather than the outer armor face itself.

A practical build recipe many competitive plastic-antweights converge on: a PLA+ or PETG structural shell for stiffness and mounting rigidity, at generous wall thickness (2.5-4mm) and moderate-to-high infill, wrapped or capped with a TPU 95A compliant skin at the highest-impact-risk faces, with small PA6/nylon parts for hinges/latches that need to flex repeatedly, and PA-CF or plain filament used sparingly for load-bearing internal ribs where stiffness matters more than impact resistance.
