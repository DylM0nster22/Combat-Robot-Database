What Survives a 1 lb Vertical Spinner Hit

Impact Survival

antweightplastic-antweightspinnerimpact-survivalcomparison

A 1 lb vertical spinner (bar or disc, typically 20,000-35,000 RPM tip speed weapons) delivers a very short-duration, very high peak-force impact concentrated on a small contact area — a fundamentally different loading condition from a slow bend test, which is why datasheet tensile strength alone is a poor predictor of what survives.

Materials that generally survive a solid hit:

  • Titanium Grade 5, 0.8mm+: dents or, at worst, develops a crack line, but rarely fails catastrophically on a single hit. Repeated hits to the same spot are its real weakness.
  • UHMW-PE, 3mm+: absorbs the blow by local plastic deformation; a spinner tooth may gouge a groove but the panel stays intact and often the blade glances off due to UHMW's low friction rather than biting in.
  • TPU 95A/85A skins: the panel deforms elastically around the impact and springs back; there's essentially nothing for the spinner to "catch" and shatter.
  • Thick PLA/PETG (2.5mm+, high infill) backed by a rigid frame: survives a moderate glancing hit reasonably well; a direct, square hit from a fast disc can still crack it, especially near a screw hole or sharp corner (stress concentrators).
  • Nylon/PA6 printed parts: high elongation-to-break lets them flex significantly before cracking, good for hinge/mount areas near the impact zone.

Materials that generally fail catastrophically on a solid hit:

  • Acrylic: shatters into shards at almost any thickness — near-zero elongation-to-break.
  • Carbon fiber plate: the resin matrix cracks and the laminate delaminates; goes from fully intact to structurally void in one hit.
  • Tungsten carbide (unsupported): chips/shears; fine as a small brazed insert on a tough steel body, catastrophic as a standalone plate.
  • Thin (sub-1mm) aluminum: dents or punches through outright since aluminum lacks both titanium's strength and UHMW's elasticity.
  • D2 or fully hardened tool steel edges: chip rather than deform when hit by an equally hard opponent.

The practical pattern: materials that survive share either (a) high strength combined with enough ductility to deform locally instead of cracking (titanium, nylon), or (b) low stiffness with very high elongation so they simply move out of the way of the blow and spring back (UHMW, TPU). Materials that fail share either brittleness (acrylic, CF, carbide, hardened tool steel) or simply insufficient strength for their thickness (thin aluminum). This is also why composite armor schemes — a TPU or UHMW outer skin over a rigid PLA/aluminum/titanium structural layer — are popular: the outer layer absorbs/deflects the initial impact energy while the inner layer maintains overall shape and mounting rigidity.
