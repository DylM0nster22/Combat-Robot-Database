Designing for the ten-minute pit repair

Design

antweightplastic-antweightdesignmaintenancereliability

Events run matches closer together than you expect. The design question is not "will this break" — it will — but "can I fix it in the time available".

The target. Assume ten to twenty minutes between matches, on a crowded table, with a soldering iron and whatever you brought.

What that implies:

Armour bolts on from outside. If replacing a damaged panel means dismantling the robot to reach the nuts, you will not do it in time. Two or three fasteners, accessible without removing anything else.

The weapon comes off as a unit. Motor, shaft, bearings and weapon as one assembly, so a damaged weapon is a swap rather than a rebuild. Carry a complete spare assembly if weight and budget allow, or at minimum a spare weapon already balanced.

Drive pods are replaceable. A motor mount that has broken loose should be a module you swap, not a chassis you re-glue.

Connectors, not solder joints, at module boundaries. Every place two subassemblies meet should have a connector. Soldering in the pits is possible but slow and error-prone when you are rushed.

Fasteners are consistent. If the whole robot uses M3 cap screws and one size of hex key, repairs are fast. A robot using five fastener sizes wastes time on tool changes and needs five sizes of spare.

Spares that actually matter, in order: battery, weapon, wheels, armour panels, fasteners, a drive motor, wiring. Print or buy multiples of anything that touches the opponent.

What you cannot fix in the pits: a cracked main chassis, a bent shaft with no spare, a stripped gearbox with no replacement. Design so these are either unlikely or carried as spares — because if one happens without a spare, your event is over regardless of how well the rest of the robot works.
