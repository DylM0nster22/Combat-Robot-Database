Practical weapon geometry selection: putting the tradeoffs together

Bite

antweightplastic-antweightbitemoibalancingattachmentdesign-tradeoffs

Every weapon-geometry decision covered in this topic connects back to a handful of the same underlying tensions, and a practical builder benefits from seeing them side by side rather than as isolated design choices.

Bite depth vs hit frequency vs balance. Fewer teeth (or a single asymmetric tooth) maximizes per-hit bite depth at the cost of hit frequency and, usually, a harder balancing job since the mass isn't evenly spread around the rotation axis. More teeth smooth out hit frequency and generally simplify balancing (mass spread more evenly) at the cost of shallower per-hit penetration. Neither choice changes the weapon's total stored energy for a given mass, radius, and rpm (formula-wg-tooth-count-moi-equivalence) -- it only changes how that fixed energy budget gets delivered over the course of a match. A builder chasing a single fight-ending hit against lightly-armored opponents should lean toward fewer teeth; a builder expecting to grind through multiple exchanges against well-armored opponents may prefer the steadier hit rate of more teeth.

Mass distribution vs manufacturing difficulty. The mass-toward-rim principle (formula-wg-mass-toward-rim-effect) says the same weapon weight delivers more stored energy the further out it sits -- solid disc < moderate rim-loading/annulus < thin ring/shell spinner, in roughly that order of both increasing energy-per-gram and increasing build difficulty and balancing sensitivity. A first hard-weapon build reasonably starts with a solid or lightly rim-loaded disc; a shell spinner is a better second- or third-build project once the builder has direct experience with balancing and bearing support at the easier end of that spectrum.

Attachment robustness vs simplicity. Set-screw hubs are the simplest and lightest option and are adequate for lower-power weapons or early builds, but the more energy a weapon stores (larger, faster, more rim-loaded), the more that stored energy shows up as shock torque on every hit -- which is exactly when a positive-drive interface (keyway or broached hex) stops being an optional upgrade and starts being a practical necessity to avoid recurring term-hub-slip failures.

Shaft support vs weapon width/reach. A cantilevered single-bearing shaft is simplest to build and adequate for a small, compact, lower-energy weapon, but as weapon width (drums, wide bars) or stored energy increases, the bending stress penalty of a long unsupported span (formula-wg-cantilever-shaft-bending) grows quickly, making double-bearing support (or a dead-shaft arrangement with bearings built into the weapon itself) the more durable choice.

The unifying lesson. None of these tradeoffs has a single universally correct answer -- the right choice depends on the specific opponent field, the builder's fabrication skill and available tools, and how much risk of a mid-match failure is acceptable in exchange for a harder-hitting or simpler design. What stays constant across all of them is that every geometry decision in this topic trades one of these same handful of things -- bite depth, hit frequency, balance difficulty, energy-per-gram, attachment robustness, and shaft support -- against another, and naming which specific tradeoff a given design choice is making is more useful than treating any one weapon form as simply 'better' than another.
