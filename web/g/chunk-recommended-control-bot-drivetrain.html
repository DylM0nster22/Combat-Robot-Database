Recommended Drivetrain: 1 lb Antweight Control Bot

Recommendations

antweightrecommendationcontrol-botdrivetrainantweight

A control bot (wedge, rammer, or full-body pusher) lives or dies by its ability to win sustained pushing exchanges, hold a heading under load, and stay mobile after taking a hit — so its drivetrain deserves a noticeably larger share of the weight budget than a spinner's. Recommended baseline:

  • Topology: 4WD is genuinely worth its weight cost here, more so than on any other antweight archetype, because losing traction on even one corner during a pushing match can mean losing the match outright. The common, weight-efficient way to get there on a 1 lb budget is 2 motors per side linked front-to-rear with a small GT2 belt and pulley set, rather than 4 full independent motors. A well-executed 2WD layout with excellent traction and a low center of gravity is still a completely viable, simpler alternative for builders prioritizing reliability over maximum pushing power.
  • Motors: FingerTech Silver Spark at 50:1-100:1, or a Repeat Robotics planetary-gearbox brushed/brushless motor at a comparable ratio, favoring the higher-torque end of the practical range compared to a spinner's build — a control bot wants torque margin well above the traction limit so it's never the motor, only the tires, holding back a push.
  • Wheels: This is the one drivetrain decision worth spending real weight budget on: compliant, higher-grip options like silicone-coated foam or Banebots-style compliant tread genuinely change match outcomes for a control bot, unlike a spinner where wheel choice matters less. Accept the extra weight of a grippier tire here in exchange for meaningfully more pushing force per the traction-limit formula.
  • Ground clearance: Slightly more forgiving than a spinner's — a control bot often wants a wedge or forward scoop close to the floor, so ground clearance should be set by that geometry first, with wheels and their guards built around it rather than the reverse.
  • Battery/voltage: 2S (7.4V) remains standard; 3S is less commonly needed here than for a spinner chasing top speed, since a control bot's advantage comes from torque and traction, not raw speed.

The key sizing check for a control bot, more than any other archetype, is running the traction-limit formula against the actual tire compound chosen (not foam's default lower coefficient) and making sure the drivetrain's torque comfortably clears that number with room to spare — an underpowered control bot that can be shoved backward once its wheels break traction has effectively lost its entire strategic purpose.
