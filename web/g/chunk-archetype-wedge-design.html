What makes a wedge actually work

Control

antweightplastic-antweightcontroldesign

A wedge is conceptually the simplest robot in combat robotics and one of the easiest to build badly. Four things decide whether it works, roughly in order of importance.

1. The leading edge must touch the floor. Not nearly touch. A wedge that rides two millimetres high is not a wedge; it is a ramp the opponent drives onto. Hinged forks or wedgelets solve this by floating independently of the chassis and following the floor.

2. The edge must be thin and hard. A thick edge presents a step for the opponent to climb rather than a ramp to be lifted by. Thin means the opponent's edge cannot get under yours. Hard means it stays thin after contact — a soft edge curls upward on its first hit and then you are the one being wedged.

3. The edge must be backed. A thin edge with nothing behind it folds. The usual construction is a hardened or spring-steel leading strip supported immediately behind by the chassis or by a thicker plate, so the tip cannot deflect upward.

4. The angle should be shallow. A shallow wedge lifts progressively and keeps the opponent's weight forward of your own axle. A steep wedge acts like a wall — it stops the opponent instead of lifting them, and it gives their weapon a flat face to hit.

Beyond the wedge itself: traction decides what happens once you are under. Pushing force is limited by friction, not by torque — a robot with more torque than its tyres can transmit simply spins its wheels. Soft, grippy tyres and weight over the driven wheels matter more than gear ratio.
