Mounting the Battery in a 1 lb Bot: CG, Protection, and Why a Puncture Ends Your Day

Mounting

antweightplastic-antweightmountingcgarmorimpact-protectionsafety

Battery placement in an antweight is a compromise between three goals that often pull in different directions: keeping the pack near the robot's center of gravity (CG) for predictable handling, keeping it away from the weapon and likely impact zones, and keeping it accessible enough to swap or inspect between matches.

CG placement: because the battery is one of the densest single components in a 1 lb robot, its position has an outsized effect on the bot's balance and how it drives, especially for wedge and lifter-style bots where weight distribution affects how well the wedge gets under an opponent. Most builders place the pack as close to the geometric/horizontal center of the chassis as the internal layout allows, and centered left-right, so it doesn't bias the bot's handling to one side or make it tip more easily toward one end.

Protecting the pack from the weapon and impacts is arguably more important than CG optimization, because a punctured or crushed pack is not a performance problem, it is a fire and match-ending safety problem (see the fire-risk chunk). The battery should be positioned as far as practically possible from the weapon's own moving parts and from the sides/faces of the robot most likely to take a direct hit (typically the front, where wedges and weapon strikes concentrate). Many antweight designs route the battery low and central, shielded on the vulnerable sides by the drivetrain, gearboxes, or a AR500/polycarbonate structural wall, effectively using the robot's own structure as an armor layer between the outside world and the pack.

Mounting method — velcro vs. foam vs. a printed cradle: hook-and-loop (velcro) strap or dual-lock tape is the simplest and most common method, letting a builder pull the pack for charging/inspection between matches without tools, but it provides essentially no cushioning against shock, so it's often paired with a thin foam pad underneath and around the pack to absorb vibration and blunt impact energy. A closed-cell foam wrap (sometimes called a "battery sock" in FPV/drone contexts, adapted for combat use) adds crush protection on top of vibration damping. A 3D-printed cradle sized precisely to the pack gives the most positive, repeatable location and the best resistance to the pack shifting under impact, at the cost of being sized for one specific pack footprint — swap to a different-size battery later and the cradle needs to be reprinted. Many competitive antweights combine methods: a printed or machined pocket that constrains the pack's position, with a layer of foam between the pack and the pocket walls, and a velcro or dual-lock strap holding it down against the pocket floor so it can't launch out under a hard hit.

Why a punctured pack ends your day: a pouch cell has no rigid outer casing, so a puncture directly exposes the layered foil/electrolyte structure to a short circuit and to air, either of which can trigger thermal runaway rapidly, sometimes within seconds. Unlike a mechanical failure elsewhere on the robot, there is no safe way to "finish the match" or even calmly walk the robot back to the pit once the battery bay has been breached — the correct response is to stop, isolate power, and get the pack to a safe, monitored, fireproof location immediately, which is why battery protection is treated as a safety requirement, not just a performance nice-to-have, in antweight design.
