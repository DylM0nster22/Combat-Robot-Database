The ways antweights lose that have nothing to do with the weapon

Reliability

antweightplastic-antweightreliabilitystrategy

Track why robots actually lose at an antweight event and the weapon is rarely the story. In rough order of frequency:

A fastener came loose. Vibration from any spinning weapon backs bolts out. The robot does not explode; a wheel gets loose, a motor mount shifts, and suddenly it drives in circles. Fix: blue threadlocker on everything, and a torque check between every match.

The battery was not charged. The robot works in the pits, dies in the arena. Fix: a charging routine and more packs than you think you need.

The failsafe tripped. Interference or a momentary signal loss stops the robot mid-match, and it counts out. Fix: proper antenna placement away from metal and from the battery, and a receiver that is not sharing a sagging power rail with the weapon.

Brownout on impact. The first hit causes a voltage dip, the receiver resets, and the robot is dead for the two seconds that decide the match. Fix: a capacitor across the battery leads, a separate BEC for the receiver, and connectors that cannot shake apart.

A wheel came off. Set screws on a smooth shaft back out. Fix: flat the shaft, use threadlocker, or use a positive retention method.

High-sided. The robot ends up balanced on an edge with the wheels off the floor, and is counted out undamaged. Fix: wheels that protrude beyond the chassis, low centre of gravity.

Overweight at check-in. No match at all. Fix: build to 445 g.

A wire broke at a solder joint. Vibration concentrates at the joint. Fix: strain relief, and hot glue over every joint.

The pattern: almost all of these are preventable with maintenance discipline rather than better design. A checklist run between every match — check fasteners, check the link, check the battery, check the wedge edge is straight, wiggle every connector — prevents most of them and takes three minutes.
