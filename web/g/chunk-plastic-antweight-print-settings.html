Print Settings That Survive Combat

Printing

plastic-antweightprintingsettingsdurability

Slicer settings for a decorative print and a combat-ready plastic ant part are worlds apart. The following settings reflect community-standard practice for parts expected to take real impact (chassis, weapon bodies, wedges); brackets and non-structural parts can use lighter settings to save print time and weight.

Wall count (perimeters): 5-8+, up from a typical decorative 2-3. Since the solid outer walls carry most of an impact load (far more than the infill lattice inside), combat parts intentionally run many more perimeters than a normal print. Some builders go further and print small/critical parts at essentially 100% infill by using enough walls that no internal infill pattern is needed at all.

Infill: 40-100%, gyroid or cubic pattern, up from a typical 15-20%. Gyroid infill in particular is popular in the community for combat parts because it provides fairly even strength in multiple directions, which matters when a weapon disc or wedge can be hit from an unpredictable angle. The weight cost is accepted deliberately, since (as covered in the build-spec chunks) armor/structure legitimately consumes the majority of a plastic ant's weight budget.

Layer height: 0.15-0.2mm is typical, balancing print time against slightly better layer bonding than a faster 0.28-0.3mm layer height would give (thinner layers generally bond a bit better, improving the class's biggest weak point: interlayer strength).

Print orientation is the single highest-leverage decision. Because printed plastic is much stronger along its layer lines than across them, parts should be oriented so the primary expected impact load runs parallel to the layers wherever geometry allows — for example, printing a weapon disc flat (layers stacked along the spin axis) rather than on its edge, so a radial impact loads the print in-plane rather than trying to peel layers apart.

Nozzle temperature and print speed: run at the filament manufacturer's recommended temperature (often slightly hot-end of the suggested range for PETG/ABS specifically) to maximize interlayer adhesion, even at the cost of slightly worse surface finish or more stringing — strength matters more than cosmetics for a fighting robot.

Post-processing (where the local ruleset allows it): annealing (baking below the plastic's melt point, sometimes packed in sand to control warping) can meaningfully improve a part's layer strength and heat resistance after printing, and is explicitly permitted post-processing under at least one major event's FAQ (SCAR). Acetone vapor smoothing on ABS parts can similarly weld outer layers together for extra surface toughness.
