Hammers, axes, and static weapons: head mass, moment arm, and edge prep

Weapon Forms

Not every antweight weapon spins continuously. Hammers, axes, and purely static weapons (forks, spikes, wedges) trade continuous rotational energy for either a single powerful actuated strike or no moving weapon at all, and their geometry is judged by different criteria than a spinner's.

Hammers and axes. A hammer or axe delivers energy through an actuated swing (typically driven by a motor through a gearbox, sometimes assisted by a spring or elastic element) rather than sustained rotation, so its key geometric parameters are head mass and moment arm (term-moment-arm-hammer) rather than rpm and tooth count. For a given available swing torque, a longer moment arm (pivot-to-head distance) delivers a faster-moving, harder-hitting head, but that same length increases the weapon's MOI with the square of the distance, demanding more torque or a longer wind-up time to reach full swing speed, and concentrates more bending stress on the pivot shaft and its bearings at the moment of impact. Head mass and moment arm are therefore sized together against the available motor/gearbox torque and the desired strike energy, generally erring toward a shorter, heavier head over a longer, lighter one when pivot/shaft durability is a concern, since the pivot sees the full reaction force of every strike.

Static weapons: forks, spikes, and wedges. These have no independent drive of their own -- their 'weapon' is a shaped, hardened piece of structure that relies on the robot's own drivetrain to push it into an opponent, get underneath them, or puncture their armor on contact. Their geometry centers on:

  • Angle. A shallow, low-angle wedge or fork tine gets under an opponent more easily and with less driving force, but provides less lift and can be more prone to riding up onto an opponent's surface rather than penetrating; a steeper angle needs more driving force to engage but can generate more lift/control once it does bite.
  • Hardness. Wedge and fork edges are usually hardened (or use a hardened insert/strip along the leading edge, following the same insert-vs-monolithic logic as spinner teeth) so they resist deforming or dulling from repeated contact with an opponent's armor or an opposing spinner's teeth.
  • Edge prep. See term-edge-prep-static-weapon for the sharpening-angle tradeoff between an aggressive, easily-penetrating thin edge and a robust, hit-tolerant thicker edge -- static weapons that expect to face opposing spinners generally favor the tougher, thicker edge, since they cannot dodge repeated hard strikes the way a mobile weapon's edge geometry might otherwise allow for.

Static weapons are attractive at 1 lb specifically because they carry no weapon motor, ESC, or spinning-mass weight budget at all, letting that entire allocation go to drivetrain, armor, or battery -- at the cost of having no independent offensive capability if the opponent avoids the geometry entirely (e.g., driving around a fork rather than into it).
