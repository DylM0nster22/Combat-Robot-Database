Weapon and Drive Motor Selection for Plastic Ants

Build Specs

plastic-antweightbuild-specsmotorsescweapon-design

Motor selection in plastic ant follows the same electronics landscape as standard antweight (motors are exempt from the plastic-only rule), but the sizing philosophy differs because the weapon it drives has a lower burst strength ceiling.

Drive motors: small brushed gearmotors dominate, most commonly the N20-size micro metal gearmotor family (roughly 10g each, available in gear ratios from crawler-slow to sprint-fast) or a 130-size can motor paired with a small gearbox. A control/wedge bot generally wants a lower, torquier gear ratio to maximize pushing power; a spinner-focused build can run a faster ratio since it relies on its weapon rather than raw ramming force to win exchanges.

Weapon motors: small brushless outrunners in the 1811-2205 size class, run on 2S, are the standard choice. Kv selection is the key design lever: roughly 2600-2800kv suits light vertical spinner discs, prioritizing RPM within the printed disc's survivable range, while 1400kv suits heavier drums/horizontal weapons, prioritizing torque to spin up a heavier printed weapon body without stalling, at a lower top RPM that's kinder to the weapon's burst strength.

ESC pairing: drive motors typically run off a combo RX+dual-brushed-ESC board (saving weight over separate receiver and ESC units), while the weapon motor needs its own small brushless ESC (roughly 4-6g, 6A-class is usually sufficient for these small outrunners) since a brushed combo board can't drive a brushless motor.

Why plastic ants don't chase the highest-kv motors available: unlike metal antweight, where a builder can push a hardened steel disc to very high RPM because the disc can survive it, a plastic ant's practical RPM ceiling is set by the printed weapon's burst strength, not the motor or ESC's capability. Overdriving a light printed disc past its survivable RPM is a common beginner mistake that results in the weapon flying apart (sometimes literally, as shrapnel) on its very first spin-up test, well before the robot ever reaches a match. Community practice is to start conservative on kv/voltage and incrementally test spin-up rather than assuming the motor's rated RPM is a safe operating target for the printed weapon attached to it.
