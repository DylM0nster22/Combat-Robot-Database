Balancing: why it matters, static vs dynamic balance, and hobbyist methods

Balancing

antweightplastic-antweightbalancingbearingsvibrationfailure-modes

An unbalanced weapon is one of the most common and most destructive problems in antweight combat robotics, and one of the cheapest to prevent with basic care during weapon fabrication.

Why an unbalanced weapon destroys bearings and shakes a bot apart. Any leftover offset between a weapon's center of mass and its rotation axis creates a centrifugal shaking force that reverses direction every revolution and is transmitted straight into the shaft and bearings. formula-wg-balance-tolerance quantifies this: the shaking force scales with the unbalance (mass times offset) times the square of angular velocity, so a residual unbalance that feels negligible at low rpm (a 150 g disc off by just 0.1 g at 30 mm produces about 13 N of shaking force at 20,000 rpm) becomes four times worse if rpm doubles. Sustained over a match, this cyclic loading is a primary driver of the term-exploded-bearing failure mode, works component-hub-set-screw joints loose (contributing to term-hub-slip), fatigues frame fasteners and welds, and can visibly shake wiring and other hardware loose elsewhere in the robot -- an unbalanced weapon doesn't just wear out its own bearings, it beats up the whole robot.

Static vs dynamic balance for a hobbyist. term-static-balance -- getting the center of mass onto the rotation axis -- is sufficient for thin, single-plane weapons like most antweight discs and shell spinners, and is well within reach of a hobbyist with basic tools. term-dynamic-balance matters once a weapon has real length along its rotation axis (long bars, wide drums), where a part can be perfectly statically balanced yet still rock/wobble because mass distribution differs between its two ends; hobbyist-level dynamic balancing usually means treating each end or plane of the weapon as its own static-balance problem (weighing and matching each half of a bar independently, for example) rather than true instrumented multi-plane balancing.

Balancing on a mandrel. The standard hobbyist method (term-balancing-mandrel): mount the weapon on its own shaft or a precision mandrel, rest it on a pair of level, low-friction supports (knife edges, a spare set of bearings, even parallel round rods), and let gravity do the work -- the heavy side will settle to the bottom. Mark that spot, correct it, spin the part to a new random orientation, and repeat until the weapon stays wherever it's placed with no consistent heavy spot.

Drilling vs adding mass. Once the heavy spot is found, either remove material from it (commonly drilling shallow holes into the heavy side, being careful not to weaken a structurally critical area like a tooth root or hub) or add a small amount of mass to the opposite (light) side (a dab of epoxy with embedded metal shot, a small bolted-on weight, or extra material in a non-critical area). Removing material is usually preferred when there's mass to spare in a non-critical zone, since it doesn't add weight to an already weight-constrained 1 lb build; adding mass is used when the light side has no safe material left to remove from, or when the imbalance is being corrected on a nearly-finished, weight-budget-critical part.

Tolerance targets. There's no single universal number, but as a practical hobbyist guideline, aim to get residual unbalance low enough that the weapon shows no consistent 'heavy side' settling behavior on a mandrel check by eye, and treat any weapon intended to run above roughly 15,000-20,000 rpm as needing noticeably tighter balance than a slower sub-10,000 rpm design, per the rpm-squared scaling in formula-wg-balance-tolerance -- when in doubt, spend the extra ten minutes balancing before the weapon's first full-speed run, since diagnosing vibration-driven bearing wear after the fact is far more time-consuming than preventing it.
