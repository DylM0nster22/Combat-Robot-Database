Weight budgeting and center-of-gravity math for a 1 lb chassis

Weight & CG Fundamentals

antweightplastic-antweightweight-and-cgfundamentals

Every antweight design starts with splitting the 454 g total weight limit into subsystem budgets (formula-weight-budget-allocation): weight_component = total_weight * pct/100. A metal-weapon vertical spinner typically runs weapon 30% / drive 20% / armor 30% / electronics 12% / fasteners 8%, while a plastic-antweight build shifts hard toward armor/frame (weapon 15% / drive 20% / armor 40% / electronics 15% / fasteners 10%) because non-metal structure needs more mass for the same stiffness.

Center of gravity (CG) height, relative to track width or wheelbase, sets tipping resistance (formula-cg-tipping-angle): theta_tip = atan((track_or_wheelbase/2)/h_cg). A low, wide chassis (90mm track, 25mm CG height) resists tipping to nearly 61 degrees of tilt; a tall, narrow one (60mm track, 40mm CG height) tips at just under 37 degrees -- a huge practical difference in how easily a side hit, a hard turn, or an opponent's wedge can flip the robot over. This same math underlies both wedge defense (a low-CG robot is harder to flip up and over a wedge) and the gyro-dance tipping-moment check elsewhere on this page (a wider/lower robot has a higher tipping-moment threshold for the same reason).

Self-righting shapes (formula-self-righting-shell-condition) use a simplified rule of thumb for domed/shell designs: keeping the CG height below the shell's own radius (h_cg < R_shell) tends to make gravity roll the robot back onto its wheels if flipped, rather than leaving it resting stably upside-down. This is a rough qualitative check, not a rigorous stability proof -- real self-righting also depends on shell uniformity, weapon drag during the roll, and floor friction.

Sheet/panel weight (formula-sheet-weight-per-area, weight = areathicknessdensity) is the workhorse calculation for checking any armor or frame stock choice against the weight budget before cutting it: a 100 cm^2 panel in 6mm HDPE (density 0.95 g/cm^3) weighs 57 g, while the same area in 3mm aluminum (density 2.70 g/cm^3) weighs 81 g despite being half the thickness -- illustrating why plastic needs roughly double the thickness to be structurally competitive, but can still sometimes land at a comparable or lower weight once thickness is matched to the actual stiffness requirement rather than compared at equal thickness.
