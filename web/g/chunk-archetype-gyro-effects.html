Gyroscopic effects and how to live with them

Spinners

antweightplastic-antweightspinnerphysicsdriving

A spinning weapon is a gyroscope, and it will fight you every time you turn.

The physics is simple. A weapon of inertia I spinning at angular velocity omega_w, in a robot turning at omega_t, produces a precession torque:

torque = I omega_w omega_t

acting about the axis perpendicular to both. For a vertical spinner that torque tries to roll the robot — it lifts one wheel. Lift a wheel, and the robot loses traction on that side, turns further, precesses harder, and lands. Repeat, and the robot appears to walk sideways across the arena. That is gyro dance.

Worked example: a 120 g, 90 mm disc has I = 1.2e-4 kg·m². At 20,000 rpm (2094 rad/s), turning at 180 deg/s (3.14 rad/s), precession torque is about 0.8 N·m. For a 454 g robot that is enough to unload a wheel comfortably.

What to do about it:

  • Turn gently. Precession scales linearly with turn rate. Smooth, wide turns cost almost nothing; snap turns cost you the wheel.
  • Lower the weapon's inertia, or accept lower rpm. Both reduce the effect proportionally.
  • Widen the wheelbase and lower the centre of gravity. This does not reduce the torque, but it raises the torque needed to actually lift a wheel.
  • Use it. A vertical spinner's precession can be used deliberately to hop the robot back onto its wheels, and experienced drivers steer partly with it.

Horizontal spinners precess too, but the torque tries to pitch the robot nose-up or nose-down rather than roll it, which is usually less disruptive — though it does change your ground clearance mid-turn, which matters a great deal for an undercutter.
