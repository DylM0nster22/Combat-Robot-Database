Why driving beats design at antweight

Driving

antweightplastic-antweightdrivingstrategygetting-started

The single most common mistake a new antweight builder makes is believing the robot decides the match. At 1 lb it mostly does not. Here is why.

The robots are close in capability. Antweight is a mature class with well-known parts. Most competitive robots in a bracket have similar motors, similar batteries and similar energy. The spread in driver skill is far wider than the spread in robot quality.

Engagement is a driving problem. Bite depends on closing speed. Getting under depends on approach angle. Avoiding a horizontal's sweep depends on timing. Every one of those is the driver, not the robot.

Matches are decided in the first five seconds. The box rush — charging before the opponent's weapon is up — wins an enormous share of antweight matches, and both executing it and defending it are pure driving.

Damaged robots still drive. A robot that has lost its weapon can still win on control if the driver knows how. A robot with a dead side can still be driven in circles well enough to survive to a decision. Both require practice you can only get by driving.

What to actually practise, in order of value:

  1. The first three seconds. Set up, count down, and execute your opening. Do it fifty times.
  2. Driving inverted. Flip the robot over and drive the same course. Use your transmitter's invert switch so the controls make sense.
  3. Angled approaches. Never drive straight at anything. Practise arriving at 30-45 degrees, which is where bite and wedge geometry both favour you.
  4. One side dead. Unplug a drive motor and learn to steer on one side. You will need this.
  5. Wall work. Pinning, carrying and pushing out all use the arena. Learn where the walls help you.

A cheap robot with a practised driver beats an expensive robot with an unpractised one, reliably, at this weight class.
