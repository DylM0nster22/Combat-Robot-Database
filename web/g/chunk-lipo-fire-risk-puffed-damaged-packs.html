LiPo Fire Risk, Puffed Packs, and Packs Damaged by a Hit

Safety

antweightplastic-antweightsafetyfire-riskpuffed-packimpact-damage

LiPo and LiHV cells store a lot of chemical energy in a very thin, mechanically fragile pouch, and that combination is the source of essentially all LiPo fire incidents: puncture, crush damage, severe overcharge, or an internal short from age/manufacturing defect can trigger thermal runaway, a self-sustaining exothermic reaction that releases the cell's stored energy rapidly as heat and, often, fire. One widely repeated comparison in RC and combat robotics safety writeups is that the energy density of a LiPo pack is in the same rough order of magnitude as TNT per unit mass — not because LiPo burns like an explosive, but because the amount of stored energy per gram is genuinely large and should be respected. Most LiPo fires happen during charging (which is why an unattended charging pack is the single most common cause) or immediately after physical damage.

Puffed packs — visibly swollen pouches — are the most common warning sign a builder will see directly. Swelling means gas has built up inside the pouch from internal chemical breakdown, which happens from overcharging, over-discharging, age, or damage. A puffed pack should never be charged, discharged normally, or "one more match" risked on it: treat it as compromised and retire it immediately. Do not attempt a charger's "recovery" or "trickle" mode on a puffed pack.

A hit that damages the battery bay is one of the most serious failure modes in antweight combat specifically, because the whole point of the class is deliberately smashing small, densely packed robots into each other. A hard hit that crushes, dents, or punctures the battery — even if the pack still reads a normal voltage afterward and the robot still drives — can have compromised the internal cell structure invisibly, creating a short that may not manifest as heat or smoke until minutes later, often while the bot is sitting in a pit box or a carry case. The standard safe practice is: after any hit that reaches the battery compartment, physically remove and inspect the pack before doing anything else with the robot — do not just check that it still powers on. If the pack shows any deformation, a punctured pouch, an exposed foil layer, unusual heat, or a chemical smell, isolate it immediately in a fireproof/LiPo-safe bag or a metal container away from other batteries, vehicles, and flammable material, and let it fully off-gas or cool in a safe outdoor location before disposal. Never put a suspect pack back in your pit box, bag, or car.

Event and venue rules at most organized combat events require charging to happen only in a designated charging area, often with a LiPo-safe bag or ammo-can-style container required under each charger, and require damaged packs to be reported and isolated rather than quietly discarded — check the specific event's rules packet, since requirements vary by organization and venue (some venues require this due to fire-marshal/insurance rules, not just robotics-club preference).
