Building a Power Budget: Current Draw, Match Drain, and Target Capacity

Power Budget

A useful power budget for an antweight starts by estimating current draw separately for drive and weapon, then combining them.

Drive current: a typical small antweight brushed gearmotor pulls roughly 1-3A under normal driving load, spiking to 5-8A when stalled against a wall or an opponent's push. With two drive motors, typical combined drive draw is therefore about 2-6A, with brief stall spikes to 10-16A if both motors stall at once (rare, but worth padding for).

Weapon current: highly dependent on motor and weapon geometry, but a common antweight brushless outrunner running a disc or drum draws roughly 3-10A once spun up and cutting normally, with sharp, brief spikes well above that at initial spin-up or when the weapon bites into an opponent and briefly bogs down.

Combined typical draw: for a moderately aggressive antweight spinner+drive combo, a combined typical draw around 8-15A is a reasonable planning number, with instantaneous spikes into the 20-30A range during simultaneous weapon spin-up and hard driving.

What a 3-minute match actually drains: using the match-runtime formula in reverse, a combined average draw of ~10A over a 3-minute (180 second = 3 minute) match works out to 10A x (3/60) hours = 0.5Ah = 500mAh consumed — notably more than the nominal capacity of a 300-450mAh pack. This is not a contradiction: real matches are not fought at full weapon+drive draw for the entire 3 minutes (there are pauses, positioning, and lulls), so actual average draw over the whole match is usually well under the peak combined figure. But it does explain why builders don't simply match pack mAh to "current draw x 3 minutes" using worst-case numbers — that would demand an unrealistically large, heavy pack.

Target capacity in practice: the widely repeated antweight rule of thumb is to estimate your realistic average-draw energy need for the match (using nominal, not worst-case, average current) and then round the capacity up 25-50% over that bare-minimum number for margin — covering pack aging, an extra practice run before a battery swap, and the fact that voltage (not just remaining mAh) needs to stay high enough to avoid sag late in the pack's discharge curve. In concrete terms, this is exactly why 300-550mAh packs (rather than the bare ~200mAh a pure energy calculation might suggest for a single 3-minute match) dominate the antweight market: the extra capacity is buying sag margin and multi-run flexibility, not just runtime.
