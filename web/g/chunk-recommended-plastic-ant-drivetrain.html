Recommended Drivetrain: Plastic Antweight

Recommendations

plastic-antweightrecommendationplastic-antweightdrivetrainbudget

Plastic antweight classes (popularized as an entry-level, low-cost, low-damage-rate scale, generally run at the same 1 lb / 454g total weight as a standard antweight but requiring the robot's structure to be built primarily from plastic rather than metal) put a premium on low cost and simple, printable/hand-buildable parts over absolute competitive performance. Note that the plastic-only requirement almost always targets the robot's structure/armor, not its internal drive components — metal-geared motors are still legal even in a strict plastic build, but budget-minded builders in this class often gravitate toward the cheapest available motors anyway, which happen to be the same N-series and servo options with the internal-quality caveats already noted. Recommended baseline:

  • Motors: For a first build or a very tight budget, a pair of modified (or factory) continuous-rotation 9g servos is the simplest possible complete drive system — no ESC needed, direct receiver connection, and matches the class's low-cost ethos exactly. For a step up in speed and torque while staying cheap, a pair of N20 gearmotors (100:1-150:1 at 6V is a reasonable starting ratio range) is the most common upgrade path — but given the well-documented quality-lottery risk in this motor family, buy at least one or two spares per side and bench-test every unit (checking free-run current and no-load RPM) before final installation, rather than trusting an untested motor straight out of the bag for an event.
  • Wheels: 3D-printed TPU wheels are close to a perfect match for this class: essentially free, fully custom to the chassis design, printable with the hub built in to save an extra part and its weight, and tough enough to survive typical plastic-antweight-level impacts. A simple o-ring tire on a printed hub is a good lightweight alternative when a builder wants more grip than bare TPU without the complexity of casting silicone.
  • Topology: 2WD live-axle drive, kept as simple as possible — this class rewards a build that's quick and cheap to repair between matches over one with maximum drivetrain sophistication, and a straightforward direct-drive 2WD layout is the easiest to fix with commonly available parts (a spare N20 or servo, a printed wheel) between rounds.
  • Ground clearance/guards: Since armor is plastic and generally thinner/lighter than a metal-armored standard antweight, keeping wheels tucked inboard behind whatever armor the design does carry matters just as much here, if not more, since plastic armor may not stop as much as metal would if an opponent gets under an exposed wheel.

Overall, the plastic antweight drivetrain recommendation prioritizes cost, simplicity, and repairability over raw performance metrics — the class exists specifically to lower the barrier to entry, and a drivetrain built around cheap, easily replaceable parts (spare N20s, printed wheels) serves that goal better than chasing the same Silver-Spark-and-Banebots spec sheet a competitive metal antweight would use.
