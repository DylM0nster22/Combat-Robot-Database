Vertical disc geometry: diameter, thickness, and tooth count

Weapon Forms

antweightplastic-antweightvertical-discgeometrytooth-count

A vertical disc spinner is, geometrically, the simplest antweight weapon: a flat disc (or near-disc) rotating in a vertical plane on a horizontal shaft, striking with its rim. Nearly every design decision comes down to three linked choices: diameter, thickness, and tooth count.

Diameter vs thickness. For a fixed weapon mass, a larger diameter pushes mass further from the axis (raising MOI and, at a given rpm, tip speed and stored energy -- see formula-wg-tip-speed-vs-diameter-tradeoff) but the disc must be proportionally thinner to hold the same weight, which can leave it thin enough to flex or crack at the root. Most 1 lb antweight discs run somewhere around 55-90 mm in diameter and 4-8 mm thick when solid, though hollow/pocketed and rim-loaded designs (see formula-wg-mass-toward-rim-effect) shift material to the outer edge and can run visually larger for the same mass.

Tooth count. Antweight vertical discs are seen with 1, 2, 3, or 4 teeth. A single tooth maximizes bite depth per hit (the classic asymmetric single-tooth disc) at the cost of the roughest, most uneven hit rate and the trickiest balance job, since all the tooth mass sits on one side and must be compensated elsewhere in the disc. Two teeth (usually symmetric, opposite each other) is the most common antweight compromise: naturally balanced, half the bite depth of a single tooth at the same rpm, double the hit frequency. Three and four teeth trade further bite depth for a smoother, more constant-feeling hit rate and are more forgiving to balance since the mass is spread more evenly; four-tooth 'plus' or 'X' patterns are popular on lower-power or control-leaning builds where sheer per-hit penetration matters less than reliable, frequent contact. Critically, formula-wg-tooth-count-moi-equivalence shows that tooth count by itself does not change the disc's total stored energy -- only how that energy is delivered per hit versus how often it's delivered.

Tooth profile, rake, and inserts. See term-tooth-profile, term-rake-angle, term-hardened-insert, and term-monolithic-weapon for how the edge itself is shaped and built. A hardened steel insert bolted or pinned into a lighter aluminum or steel disc body is extremely common at antweight scale: it lets the bulk of the disc be optimized for weight and MOI while only the actual edge needs to survive direct hardened-on-hardened impacts.

Mass toward the rim. Whatever the diameter and tooth count, the single biggest lever for a harder-hitting disc at the same total weight is pushing mass outward -- thinning the web/spokes near the hub and concentrating material near the outer edge -- exactly the effect quantified in formula-wg-mass-toward-rim-effect, where reshaping a uniform disc into a rim-loaded ring of the same mass and outer radius can add 50%+ more stored energy.
