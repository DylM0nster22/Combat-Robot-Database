Core Safety Systems: Power Switches, Weapon Locks, and Radio Fail-Safes

Safety & Inspection

Three safety systems appear on essentially every rulebook's inspection checklist regardless of weight class or organization, because they address the three most common ways a combat robot can hurt someone outside the arena: an accidentally-live robot, an accidentally-spinning weapon during handling, and a robot that keeps moving after its driver loses control.

Main power disconnect / removable link: every ruleset requires a way to fully cut power to the robot's motors and weapon that doesn't require tools and can be operated quickly by the builder or a marshal. RoboGames is explicit about timing: the disconnect must be operable within 15 seconds without endangering the operator, and full deactivation of the robot must be achievable within 60 seconds. AWS (UK) simply requires it be 'externally accessible' and operable 'without tools.' Larger classes often add a secondary, more certain isolation method — a physically removable link or jumper that a marshal can pull even if a switch has failed or is hard to reach — on top of the primary switch, precisely because a bigger, more dangerous robot deserves more than one way to guarantee it goes dead.

Weapon lock: any robot with a weapon capable of moving under its own power (a spinning disc/drum, a lifting arm, a hammer) must have a physical lock fitted whenever it is powered on outside the arena — during transport, pit setup, and staging before a match. RoboGames requires this lock to be a clearly visible, high-visibility color (neon orange or similar) specifically so an inspector or bystander can tell at a glance whether a weapon is safed or live; NHRL requires a lock that prevents a spinning weapon from moving 'outside of the cage,' i.e. it must contain the weapon even if it somehow spins up unexpectedly. Locks are fitted before power-on and removed only once the robot is placed in the arena and the match is about to begin.

Radio fail-safe: the receiver/ESC system must be programmed so that losing the transmitter signal (turned off, out of range, battery dies, interference) causes all motion — drive and weapon alike — to stop immediately, rather than continuing at whatever the last received command was (which could mean a weapon left spinning at full speed or a drivetrain left driving into a wall or an official). This is tested directly at inspection: an inspector or the driver deliberately powers off or unplugs the transmitter while the robot is live, and watches to confirm the robot goes fully inert. NHRL calls this out as a mandatory pass/fail test every robot must clear before competing; AWS requires the same behavior ('robot shall cease all motion when the controlling transmitter is switched off') as a hard rule.

Together these three systems form the backbone of virtually every technical inspection checklist in the sport: an inspector will check for a functioning, accessible power disconnect, a properly colored and fitted weapon lock, and a passing fail-safe test, typically in that order, before moving on to weight, dimensions, and weapon-specific checks.
