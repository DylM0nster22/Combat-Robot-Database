Worked Example: Stall Current, ESC Ratings, and Duty-Cycle Heating

Formulas

Every drive motor draws its highest current right at stall (zero RPM, full voltage applied) — this is the number an ESC, wiring, connectors, and battery all need to be sized to survive, at least briefly, even though the robot won't spend most of a match sitting at true stall.

Worked example: A 2WD antweight running two FingerTech Silver Sparks, each rated 1.3A stall current at 7.4V. Worst case, both motors stall simultaneously (e.g. the robot is pinned against a wall while still commanding full throttle): I_stall_total = 2 x 1.3A = 2.6A combined. Normal cruising current while driving freely is much lower, typically in the 0.2-0.6A-per-motor range, since a motor only draws current in proportion to the torque it's actually producing, not its theoretical maximum.

Sizing rule of thumb: an ESC or motor driver's continuous current rating should sit comfortably above the expected cruising current (so it runs cool through normal driving), while its burst/peak current rating needs to clear the full per-channel stall current with some safety margin — for a 1.3A-stall motor, a driver rated for 2-3A continuous per channel is typically adequate, which is why most small antweight brushed ESCs (FingerTech's own boards among them) are specified in that range.

The part that trips up new builders is the duty-cycle / thermal side of this, separate from the ESC's rating entirely: these tiny motors have very little thermal mass, so even a stall current well within the ESC's rated capability can still cook the motor's own windings if held for too long, because resistive (I²R) heating in the coil accumulates faster than the small motor can shed it. As a rough guide, holding a Silver Spark-class motor at full stall for more than about 10-20 seconds starts to risk winding insulation damage, even though the ESC itself may show no signs of distress. This is why experienced drivers are taught to "bump" the throttle — short pulses of full stall rather than holding it — during a pushing match or when momentarily jammed against an obstacle, treating the stall current rating as a brief-duration limit rather than something the motor can sustain indefinitely.
