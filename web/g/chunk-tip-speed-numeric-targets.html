Why more speed is not free. Kinetic energy scales with the square of angular velocity (KE = 0.5Iomega^2), so it is tempting to chase maximum RPM for maximum stored energy. Two things push back hard against that instinct. First, aerodynamic drag power scales roughly with the cube of tip speed (drag force scales with velocity squared, and power is force times velocity, so power to overcome drag scales as v^3) -- going from 200 mph to 400 mph tip speed does not just double the power needed to sustain that speed against air resistance, it roughly multiplies it by 8x, which very quickly exceeds what a small antweight motor and battery can supply continuously, especially on a horizontal bar or drum with much more frontal area than a thin disc. Second, higher RPM for the same weapon geometry means less bite (see term-bite): the weapon spends less time in contact with the opponent's armor per revolution, so beyond a certain point extra speed increasingly produces glancing, superficial hits rather than deeper, more damaging engagement.

The practical result is that the antweight community has converged on a fairly narrow effective band -- roughly 180-260 mph (80-116 m/s) for vertical discs, generally lower for horizontal bars and drums that carry more drag and are tuned for bite over raw speed -- rather than simply maximizing RPM. Builders selecting a weapon motor should treat "highest KV I can fit" as actively counterproductive past this band, and instead spend the extra motor/battery capacity on torque (lower KV, bigger stator, or a belt reduction) to improve spin-up time and bite at a fixed, sensible tip speed target.
