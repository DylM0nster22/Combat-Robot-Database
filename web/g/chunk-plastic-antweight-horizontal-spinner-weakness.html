Why Horizontal Spinners Behave Differently in Plastic

Meta

plastic-antweightmetahorizontal-spinnerphysicsweapon-design

In standard metal antweight, a horizontal (bar) spinner with a hardened tool-steel or titanium tip is one of the most feared weapons in the class: a thin, hard edge concentrates enormous force into a tiny contact area, which is exactly what lets it punch through an opponent's aluminum or polycarbonate armor. In plastic antweight, the exact same geometry becomes one of the weakest weapon choices, for reasons rooted directly in how FDM-printed plastic fails.

The tip has to be plastic. The rules require the weapon's striking surface to be one of the approved printable plastics — no hardened steel edge is legal. A thin cross-section is the worst possible shape for a printed part because it has the least material spread across the load path and the fewest print layers bonded together resisting a perpendicular impact.

Layer-line anisotropy compounds the problem. FDM parts are always weaker across their layer lines than along them ('3D printed parts will always have more strength parallel to their layers as opposed to perpendicular,' as community build guides consistently note). A thin spinning bar takes repeated shock loading at exactly the orientation most likely to peel layers apart at the tip, so bar spinners in this class are disproportionately prone to snapping their own tip off on an early hit — sometimes on the very first swing against a solid target.

The physics also work against it. Kinetic energy in a spinning weapon scales with mass and the square of tip speed (KE = 1/2 I omega^2). Because printed plastics are roughly 3-6x less dense than steel, matching a metal bar's stored energy within the same weight budget means either much higher RPM (which raises tip stress and burst risk further) or a physically much larger/thicker bar (which then behaves more like a drum, undercutting the whole point of a thin cutting bar).

Profile and stability costs. A bar spinner also typically sits higher and presents a larger flat vertical profile than a low drum or vertical disc, which raises the robot's center of gravity and gives control bots and wedges an easier target to get under or flip.

Net effect on the meta: builders who bring a metal-ant bar-spinner mindset into plastic ant consistently find it underperforms drums and vertical spinners, which spread the same kinetic energy across a wider, lower-stress contact area (a disc rim or drum wall) instead of concentrating it at one thin tip — turning 'cutting' into 'blunt ramming,' which is what plastic can actually survive doing to someone else.
