ESC sizing and current headroom for weapon motors

Drivetrain Layout

antweightplastic-antweightesccurrent-headroomweapon-esc

A weapon ESC needs to be sized around the motor's realistic stall/spin-up current, not its steady-state cruise current, since spin-up and post-impact recovery are when current peaks hardest. The commonly cited rule of thumb is to choose an ESC rated for at least 20% more continuous current than the motor's expected max current draw at your chosen voltage, to leave margin for the FET/driver stage's own heating and for brief current spikes during hard stalls that exceed the motor's steady rating.

Weapon ESCs differ from drive ESCs in one important way: many stock FPV racing/freestyle ESCs run soft-start firmware logic intended to protect propellers and smooth throttle response, which can actively fight a weapon's need to slam to full throttle and recover instantly from a dead stop after a stall. Purpose-built or reconfigured weapon ESCs (commonly running AM32 or BLHeli-family firmware with weapon-specific settings, or dedicated combat ESCs sold by hobby vendors) disable or minimize this soft-start behavior and raise or disable low-RPM current limiting so the weapon can recover from a stall as fast as the motor and battery physically allow.

It's also worth understanding what an ESC's amp rating actually means: a "35A ESC" is a rating for how much current the ESC's FETs and firmware can reliably pass without overheating or failing, not a hard current limit the ESC actively enforces on the motor. Actual current draw is set by the motor's load (locked-rotor/stall current is set by applied voltage and winding resistance, I = V/R, clamped by whatever the battery and wiring can deliver) -- if a motor's real stall current under your battery and voltage exceeds the ESC's rating, the ESC is what fails, typically by cooking its FETs, not the motor throttling itself back automatically. This is why builders pairing a low-resistance, high-stall-current motor (common in the bigger 25xx-28xx sizes) with a small-antweight-class ESC need to actually calculate or measure real stall current rather than assuming the ESC's rating alone protects the system.
