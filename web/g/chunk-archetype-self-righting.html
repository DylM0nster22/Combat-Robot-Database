Self-righting: invertibility versus a mechanism

Design

antweightplastic-antweightdesignreliability

Every antweight will end up on its back. There are three answers, and at 1 lb only two are sensible.

Be invertible. Build the robot so being upside down does not matter: wheels that protrude above and below the chassis, a weapon that works in both orientations, and an invert switch on the transmitter that reverses steering. This costs essentially nothing in weight and is the default choice at antweight. The main design constraints are that the wheels must stick out both ways — which exposes them to undercutters — and the weapon arc must clear in both orientations.

Use the weapon. A vertical spinner or a drum can be used to flip the robot back over by firing the weapon against the floor and letting the reaction torque roll the robot. This is free — the mechanism already exists — but it is unreliable on a smooth floor and needs the weapon to be undamaged, which is exactly what it may not be when you are upside down.

Carry a srimech. A dedicated arm, ram or lever whose only job is righting. At 1 lb this typically costs 8-15% of the weight budget for a function that invertibility provides for free. It is justified mainly when the archetype cannot be invertible — a wedge with a fixed low edge, a lifter with an arm that only works one way up.

The practical recommendation for antweight: design for invertibility from the start. It is much harder to retrofit than to plan for, and it removes an entire category of match-ending failure. If your archetype genuinely cannot be invertible, budget the srimech weight honestly rather than hoping the weapon will do it.
