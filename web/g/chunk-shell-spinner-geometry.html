Shell spinner geometry: ring construction, wall thickness, and support

Weapon Forms

antweightplastic-antweightshell-spinnerringgeometrymoi

A shell spinner takes the mass-toward-the-rim principle to its logical extreme: instead of a solid disc or a thick-walled drum, the entire weapon is a thin ring (a 'shell') with as much of its mass as possible pushed to the outer diameter, sometimes with the drive motor and structure sitting entirely inside the ring's diameter.

Why shells hit so hard for their weight. formula-wg-mass-toward-rim-effect shows that a thin ring (I = mR^2) carries exactly double the MOI -- and therefore double the stored kinetic energy at the same rpm -- of a uniform solid disc of the identical mass and outer radius (I = 0.5m*R^2). This is the core reason shell spinners have a reputation for punching above their weight class: for the same total weapon mass, concentrating essentially all of it at maximum radius roughly doubles the energy budget compared to a solid-disc weapon of the same size.

Wall thickness and ring construction. The ring itself is usually a hardened steel or steel-toothed band, thin enough that essentially all its mass sits at (or very near) the outer radius. Thinner walls push the MOI advantage further but reduce the ring's own structural stiffness, making it more prone to flexing, ovaling, or cracking under impact unless well supported. Some shell spinners integrate teeth directly into the ring's edge (effectively a hardened, toothed hoop); others bolt a separate ring of tooth inserts onto a plain structural shell.

Support ring and bearings. Because the ring is thin and its mass is concentrated far from center, it depends heavily on term-support-ring-shell-spinner -- a large-diameter bearing, a machined bearing race, or a set of rollers/guide bearings riding the inside of the ring -- to stay round and running true at speed, in addition to whatever central drive bearing turns it. A shell that relies only on a small central bearing and no outer support is much more prone to wobble, out-of-round running, and the resulting vibration/bearing wear described in the balancing section, especially as ring diameter grows relative to its wall thickness.

Practical antweight scale. At 1 lb, shell spinners are a more advanced, higher-effort build than a solid disc: the thin ring, precise support-bearing arrangement, and tighter balance tolerance (driven by the higher stored energy per gram of weapon material) all demand more careful machining and assembly than a simpler solid or rim-loaded disc, but reward that effort with meaningfully more kinetic energy for the same weapon weight budget.
