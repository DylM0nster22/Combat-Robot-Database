European and Other International Ruleset Variants

Major Rulesets

Outside the US (SPARC/NHRL/RoboGames/BattleBots) and UK (AWS) rulesets, several other regional variants exist, generally built on the same underlying safety philosophy but with local adjustments for weight classes, allowed weapon types, or historical quirks.

Fighting Robots Association (FRA), based in the UK, publishes its own build rules that are referenced across a number of UK and continental European events, generally complementing AWS's antweight-specific rules with guidance for a broader span of weight classes. FRA-affiliated documentation also circulates through European hobbyist mirrors (for example, a French robot-combat community site hosting an FRA build-rules PDF), reflecting how UK-style rules have propagated into continental European club scenes that don't have their own from-scratch rulebook.

Germany has historically taken a notably different approach to one specific banned-weapon category: rather than banning entanglement weapons outright the way SPARC, RoboGames, and AWS do, German events (associated with RoboGames-adjacent competition) have permitted entanglement devices (nets, tangling weapons) in a separate, clearly labeled 'net-fighting' side bracket kept apart from mainstream weight-class combat. This is the most commonly cited example of a national/regional ruleset carving out an explicit exception to an otherwise near-universal ban, rather than simply having laxer rules across the board.

Australia, via the RoboWars Australia community, is a useful counter-example to the assumption that 'international rules always mean lighter weight classes': it explicitly follows the SPARC ruleset and the same heavier US-style ladder rather than the UK/AWS scale — Australian Antweight is 1 lb (454 g), the same as US antweight, not the UK's 150 g class. Australia's ladder mirrors SPARC almost exactly (Antweight 1lb, Beetleweight 3lb, Mantisweight 6lb, Hobbyweight 12lb, Featherweight 30lb, Lightweight 60lb, Middleweight 120lb, Heavyweight 220lb) but adds one class the mainstream US ladder doesn't commonly run: a 340 lb (154 kg) Super Heavyweight tier above heavyweight. Non-wheeled robots get the standard 100% weight bonus at most classes, but that bonus shrinks to 35% specifically at the 340 lb Super Heavyweight level — presumably because a fully doubled 680 lb walking robot at that scale would be both impractical and disproportionately dangerous relative to the rest of the ladder. Australia also runs a distinct 'UltiBot' division for outdoor-terrain combat robots (30 kg for a mobile entrant, 25 kg for a fixed/stationary entrant), which has no equivalent in indoor-arena US/UK rules at all.

The overall pattern across international variants is consistency in the safety fundamentals (power disconnects, weapon locks, radio fail-safes, bans on liquids/explosives/jamming) with the most visible differences showing up in weight-class numbers and structure: the UK/AWS runs a much lighter, antweight-scale-and-below ladder (150 g down to 25 g) with a 50% non-roller bonus, while the US (SPARC/NHRL/RoboGames) and Australia (RoboWars) share a heavier ladder starting at 1 lb with a 100% walker bonus, and Australia extends that ladder one tier further with a 340 lb Super Heavyweight class at a reduced 35% bonus. Germany's net-fighting exception (permitting entanglement in a dedicated side bracket rather than mainstream classes) is the clearest example of a deliberate weapon-rule carve-out rather than a structural weight-class difference. A builder planning to compete internationally, or sourcing rules information from a non-domestic source, should treat both the weight-class numbers and the walker-bonus percentage as likely points of divergence, and should never assume a class name (especially 'Antweight') transfers its exact weight across borders without checking the specific ruleset.
