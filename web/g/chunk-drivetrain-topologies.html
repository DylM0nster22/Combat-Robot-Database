Drivetrain Topologies: 2WD, 4WD, Belt/Chain/Gear, Dead vs Live Axle

Drivetrain

The overwhelming majority of 1 lb antweights use 2WD direct (live-axle) drive: two drive gearmotors, one per side, each with a wheel mounted straight onto its output shaft. This is the simplest, lightest, and most repairable layout — no belts, chains, or extra shafts to fail — and it's exactly what FingerTech's extra-long Silver Spark shaft is designed around. Its main limitation is that all of the robot's weight rides on two contact points (plus perhaps a rear skid or caster), so if one wheel is lifted off the ground (climbing over an opponent's wedge lip, or after a weapon hit), the robot can lose most of its drive traction at once.

4WD layouts address that by putting a powered wheel at each corner, giving redundancy (the robot still has drive even with one wheel airborne) and more overall traction for pushing matches, at the cost of extra weight and complexity. On a 1 lb budget, running four full motors is often too heavy, so 4WD antweights frequently use a belt-driven approach instead: one motor per side, with a small GT2 timing belt and pulley set (common because GT2 hardware is cheap and widely available from the 3D-printer supply chain) linking the front and rear wheel on that side so they spin together. This gets 4-wheel traction from only 2 motors, at the cost of belt tension/wear as an extra maintenance and failure point, and the belt run needs to be shielded from direct weapon hits. Chain drive is a heavier, more robust alternative to belts seen more often on larger weight classes, and gear-driven linkages (a small gear train connecting front and rear wheel shafts) are a third option that trades some efficiency for eliminating belt tension entirely.

A separate design axis is dead axle vs live axle mounting. In a live-axle setup (the antweight default), the wheel is mounted directly to the motor's output shaft, and that shaft is what takes the wheel's structural and impact loads. In a dead axle setup, the wheel spins freely on a fixed, non-rotating shaft (supported by its own bearings), and is driven separately — typically by a sprocket, pulley, or gear fixed to the wheel and linked back to the motor by a chain, belt, or gear train. This isolates the motor's own output shaft from direct impact loads (useful in wedge or lifter bots where a wheel might take a hard hit) and lets multiple wheels share a single structural axle, but adds parts, weight, and assembly complexity that most 1 lb antweights can't easily afford — dead axles show up far more often on beetleweight and larger designs.

Finally, ground clearance and wheel guards matter more on an antweight than the raw drivetrain numbers might suggest: exposed wheels are one of the most common ways to lose a match, since a stray hit from an opposing weapon (or even the arena floor's hazards) can destroy a wheel or knock a robot immobile in seconds. Competitive builds usually keep wheels tucked fully inside the body's footprint (inboard, not protruding past the armor line) and add minimal ground clearance — just enough to clear floor screws, seams, and hazards, no more — since every extra millimeter of clearance is extra exposed wheel and extra leverage for an opponent's wedge to get underneath.
