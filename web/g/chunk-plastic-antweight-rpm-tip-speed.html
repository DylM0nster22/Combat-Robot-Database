Typical Weapon RPM and Tip Speeds Achievable in Plastic

Weapon Design

plastic-antweightweapon-designrpmtip-speedphysics

Tip speed — the linear speed of a spinning weapon's outermost point — is the standard way combat robotics talks about weapon power, and it's calculated with the formula SPARC itself uses for its speed-limited classes: Tip Speed (ft/s) = RPM x diameter (inches) x 0.00436.

Typical plastic ant weapon motor/RPM combinations: small brushless outrunners in the 1811-2205 size class are the norm, run on 2S (7.4V) LiPo. A common vertical-spinner setup pairs a roughly 2600-2800kv outrunner direct-driving (no gearing) a 2-2.5-inch printed disc, giving a free-spin RPM in the 15,000-20,000 RPM range before load, settling lower under real combat load and with the added rotational inertia of the printed disc. Plugging a conservative 12,000 RPM combat-load estimate and a 2.5-inch diameter into the tip-speed formula gives roughly 12,000 x 2.5 x 0.00436 ≈ 131 ft/s — a meaningful but not extreme tip speed compared to what a similarly-sized metal antweight spinner could sustain.

Why plastic ants tend to run lower kv/tip speed than metal ants of the same size: the practical ceiling is set by the printed disc/drum's burst strength, not the motor's capability. Community guidance repeatedly notes that 1400kv motors suit slower horizontal/drum weapons (prioritizing torque and lower stress on the weapon body) while around 2800kv suits lighter vertical spinner discs — both meaningfully lower than the highest-kv motors used in metal-ant record-chasing builds, because a printed weapon spun to the RPM a metal disc could survive would simply fly apart.

SPARC's formal tip-speed limits only bind in specific contexts: the 'Sportsman' class caps ordinary spinning weapons at 20 ft/s (with a higher allowance for saw-like weapons meeting a tooth-pitch test), and open-air combat classes cap slow spinners at the same 20 ft/s. Standard enclosed-arena plastic ant classes are not usually tip-speed-limited by the ruleset itself — the practical limit instead comes from the printed material's own failure point, which for a well-designed thick PETG disc or drum tends to land somewhere in the 80-150 ft/s range in community experience, well below what a hardened steel antweight weapon could achieve (some metal antweight spinners exceed 300-400 ft/s), and this gap is treated as a low-confidence estimate based on community reporting rather than a single authoritative source.

Note: RPM/tip-speed figures are community-sourced estimates, not from a single authoritative measurement; treat as approximate.
