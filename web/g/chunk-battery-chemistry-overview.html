Battery Chemistry Choices for Antweights: LiPo, LiHV, Li-ion, LiFePO4, NiMH

Chemistry

antweightplastic-antweightchemistrylipolihvli-ionlifepo4nimh

LiPo (lithium polymer) is the default power source for essentially every competitive 1 lb antweight built since the mid-2000s, and for good reason: it offers the best combination of energy density (roughly 150-200+ Wh/kg), discharge rate, and pouch-format packaging flexibility of any chemistry available in small sizes. A standard LiPo cell is charged to 4.20V and discharged to about 3.0-3.3V under load, giving a 3.7V nominal per cell used for all voltage-tier math (2S = 7.4V, 3S = 11.1V, 4S = 14.8V).

LiHV (high-voltage LiPo) is a chemistry variant, not just a different label — the cell construction is tuned to tolerate a higher full-charge voltage, typically 4.35V/cell (some cells go to 4.40V) instead of 4.20V. Nominal voltage for LiHV is usually quoted as 3.8V/cell rather than 3.7V. The real gain is modest but real: at the same physical cell, LiHV stores roughly 5-10% more energy than standard LiPo, and since it runs at a higher voltage across the whole discharge curve, motors and ESCs see more usable voltage right up until the pack is nearly empty — a genuine antweight advantage for spinner tip speed and drive punch late in a match. The tradeoffs: an LiHV pack must be charged on a charger or profile that explicitly supports the 4.35V/cell ceiling (charging it on a standard LiPo profile just leaves capacity on the table; overcharging a standard cell to LiHV voltages is dangerous), and LiHV cells generally show somewhat shorter cycle life at aggressive charge/discharge rates than standard LiPo.

Li-ion (18650/21700 cylindrical, e.g. Molicel) offers even higher energy density and much better cycle life than LiPo pouch cells, and is the backbone of e-bikes, power tools, and larger combat weight classes' main packs. It is essentially absent from competitive 1 lb antweight main-battery use, though, because a single round cell is too heavy relative to its usable current for the weight budget, and the rigid cylindrical format doesn't pack efficiently into a flat antweight chassis. It shows up in antweight discussions mostly as context or in bench/test setups, not in the ring.

LiFePO4 (LiFe) cells run a lower nominal voltage (3.3V/cell) and lower energy density than LiPo, but have a much flatter discharge curve, far higher abuse tolerance, and a dramatically lower fire risk. Antweight builders occasionally use a small 2S LiFe pack (6.6V nominal) as an isolated receiver/electronics power source specifically because it won't sag or brown out the way the shared main LiPo can under weapon load — not as a main weapon/drive battery.

NiMH was the default antweight chemistry before affordable LiPo took over, typically a 6-cell 7.2V stick pack. It is essentially obsolete today: roughly 1/3 the energy density of LiPo by weight and unable to sustain modern brushless weapon current without severe sag. It survives only in legacy kit documentation and exhibition/vintage classes.
