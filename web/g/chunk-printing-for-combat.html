Printing parts that survive combat

3D printing

plastic-antweightantweightprintingplastic-ant

A printed part in a combat robot fails differently from a printed part in any other application, and the profile you use for ordinary printing is close to the worst one for combat.

The core problem is anisotropy. An FDM part bonds each layer to the one below by partial remelting. Along the layers the material approaches its bulk strength; across the layers it is typically around half that. Every other recommendation follows from this.

Orientation first. Decide where the load goes before you decide anything else. A wedge wants impacts pressing layers together. A motor mount wants the bolt axis running across the layers, not along them, so the bolt cannot split the part like a log. If a part has two conflicting load paths, split it in two, print each optimally, and bolt them together.

Perimeters over infill. Stiffness and impact resistance live in the shell. Going from 2 to 5 perimeters helps a combat part far more than going from 20% to 60% infill, and costs less weight and much less time. Start at 4-6 perimeters, 4-6 solid top and bottom layers, 20-30% gyroid or cubic infill.

Print hot and slow. Nozzle temperature toward the top of the filament's range, part cooling reduced, speed reduced. All three increase the time the layer interface spends hot enough to bond. You will get a worse surface finish and a significantly stronger part. For PLA+ this trade is heavily worth it; for PETG, reduce cooling cautiously as it already bonds well and droops easily.

Bigger nozzle. 0.6 mm or 0.8 mm. Fatter extrusions mean more interface area between layers and fewer of them. Combat parts have no need of 0.4 mm detail.

Design out the crack starters. Fillet every internal corner — 1-2 mm is enough to change where a part fails. Chamfer external edges. Taper between thick and thin sections rather than stepping.

Never thread into plastic. Heat-set inserts, captive nuts, or through-bolts with nylocs. A thread cut directly in plastic survives a few assemblies and no impacts.
