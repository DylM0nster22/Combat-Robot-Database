Using TPU: the plastic ant's best armour

3D printing

plastic-antweightprintingmaterialsplastic-ant

In metal antweight you armour with titanium or UHMW and rely on strength. In plastic antweight the strongest available approach is usually the opposite: armour that deliberately deforms.

Why compliance wins. Impact force depends on how quickly momentum changes. A rigid PLA panel stops the incoming tooth almost instantly, so the peak force is enormous and the panel cracks. A flexible TPU panel deflects over several milliseconds, spreading the same momentum change over more time, so peak force drops sharply — and TPU returns most of that energy elastically instead of fracturing.

Which TPU. Shore hardness is the main variable:

  • 95A — the general-purpose choice. Stiff enough to hold a shape, soft enough to absorb. Prints reasonably on most printers.
  • 85A — much softer and more absorbent, better for pure impact panels, harder to print (needs a direct drive extruder and slow speeds).
  • 60D and harder — nearly rigid, useful for structural parts that need some give, such as motor mounts or wheel hubs.

Where to use it. Front and side armour panels, wheels and tyres, motor mount bushings, and any bracket that keeps shearing off. A common plastic ant construction is a PLA+ structural frame carrying TPU armour panels bolted on from outside — stiff where stiffness is needed, compliant where impacts land.

Printing it. Slow (20-30 mm/s), direct drive if you have it, minimal retraction, and no cooling to speak of. TPU is forgiving about layer adhesion — it bonds to itself well — which is another reason it survives hits that crack PLA.

Watch the rules. At least one organisation's plastic-class rules name TPU and nylon as disallowed, even as wheel hubs, while others permit them freely. This is one of the specific areas where plastic ant rulesets disagree, so confirm against your event's published list before you commit a design to TPU armour.
