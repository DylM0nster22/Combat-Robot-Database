Worked Example: Robot Speed from Motor RPM and Wheel Diameter

Formulas

The most basic drivetrain calculation a builder needs is converting a motor's rated speed into a robot's actual top speed on the floor. The relationship is: wheel RPM equals motor no-load RPM divided by the total gear ratio, and linear speed equals wheel RPM times the wheel's rolling circumference.

In a convenient mixed-units form: v(mph) = (motor_rpm / gear_ratio) x pi x wheel_diameter(in) / 1056. The constant 1056 converts inches-per-minute into miles-per-hour (there are 63,360 inches in a mile and 60 minutes in an hour, so 63,360/60 = 1,056).

Worked example: Take a FingerTech Silver Spark at its fastest stock ratio, 11.1:1, with a base motor no-load speed of 11,530 RPM (at 7.4V, the common 2S LiPo antweight voltage), paired with a 2.0in diameter wheel.

  1. Wheel RPM = 11,530 / 11.1 = 1,038.7 RPM
  2. v(mph) = 1,038.7 x 3.1416 x 2.0 / 1056
  3. v(mph) = 1,038.7 x 6.283 / 1056 = 6,527 / 1056 ≈ 6.18 mph

This lines up well with commonly cited real-world antweight sprint speeds of roughly 6 mph across a short arena run. It's worth being clear about what this number is and isn't: it's a theoretical no-load top speed, calculated as if the motor were spinning completely unloaded at its rated voltage forever. In practice, actual top speed on the floor comes in lower — typically 70-90% of this figure — because of three effects this simple formula ignores: (1) voltage sag, since a small LiPo's internal resistance and the motor's own current draw pull the actual voltage at the motor below the pack's rated nominal voltage under load; (2) rolling and gearbox friction losses, which this formula doesn't subtract at all (it assumes 100% efficiency); and (3) the fact that a wheel does still need a small amount of torque even at 'cruising' speed to overcome friction, meaning the motor never quite reaches the true zero-load RPM the datasheet lists. Builders typically treat the no-load formula as a solid upper bound / rough comparison tool for choosing between ratios or wheel sizes, then confirm the real number with an actual timed run once the robot is built.
