Mounting weapon motors: bolt patterns, face-mount vs clamp, why printed mounts fail

Mounting & Mechanical Integration

antweightplastic-antweightmountingbolt-pattern3d-printing

FPV motors ship with a small number of standardized rear-face bolt patterns, and knowing them is essential for designing (or printing) a weapon-motor mount:

  • 9x9mm, M2 screws -- the smallest common pattern, used on whoop-class 11xx-13xx motors.
  • 12x12mm, M2 screws -- used on many 13xx-14xx and some 18xx-20xx motors; a few 1408/1806-class motors offer both 9x9 and 12x12 mounting holes on the same face for cross-compatibility.
  • 16x16mm, M3 screws -- the de-facto standard for the antweight bread-and-butter 18xx-22xx class; most printed and machined antweight weapon-motor mounts are built around this pattern.
  • 16x19mm and 19x19mm, M3 screws -- common alternate/additional patterns on 22xx-25xx motors, sometimes offered alongside 16x16 on the same motor face for frame compatibility.
  • 25.5x25.5mm and larger, M3 -- seen on some 25xx-28xx heavy-lift motors, at the upper end of what an antweight would use.

Face-mount vs clamp mount. Nearly all FPV-derived weapon motors are face-mounted: four screws thread into blind holes on the motor's rear face, pulling the motor flush against a flat mounting plate. This is simple and lines up naturally with 3D-printed or laser-cut antweight frame plates, but it means 100% of the impact reaction load (both the weapon's radial impact force and the motor's own torque reaction) is carried by four small screws in relatively soft aluminum threads and whatever material the mounting plate is made of. Clamp-style mounts (a collar that clamps around the motor's outer can rather than threading into its face) are far less common at these small sizes -- they exist in some larger combat classes but are rare below beetleweight because there's little clean cylindrical can surface to clamp on a typical outrunner shell.

Why printed mounts fail. A 3D-printed (FDM plastic) weapon-motor mount is loaded by (a) high-frequency vibration during normal spin, which fatigues layer lines over time, and (b) sudden, large shock loads on every weapon impact, which can exceed the interlayer bond strength of FDM plastic even when the same part would easily survive the vibration alone. Common printed-mount failure patterns are: mounting screw holes stripping or elongating under repeated impact torque reaction (worse with M2 in soft plastic than M3), and the print delaminating along a layer line running roughly perpendicular to the impact load direction. Mitigations builders commonly use: orienting the print so layer lines run parallel to the expected impact force rather than perpendicular to it, printing at high infill (or solid) specifically around the mounting bolt bosses, using threaded heat-set inserts instead of printing threads or relying on self-tapping screws directly into plastic, and backing the printed mount with a thin metal plate to spread the bolt load rather than reacting it purely against plastic. Many competitive antweight builders reserve metal (aluminum or steel) motor mount plates specifically for the weapon motor even when the rest of the frame is printed, precisely because the weapon mount sees the highest instantaneous loads on the entire robot.
