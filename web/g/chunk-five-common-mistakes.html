The five most common numeric mistakes in antweight design

Common Mistakes

antweightplastic-antweightcommon-mistakesdesign-pitfalls

1. Confusing the exact Kt = 9.5493/Kv with the hobby shorthand Kt = 8.27/Kv. The hobby shorthand already assumes ~87% efficiency baked in (8.27/9.5493 = 0.866). Using the exact form and THEN separately derating for efficiency is correct; using the hobby shorthand and derating AGAIN double-counts losses and can understate real available torque by ~25% or more, leading to over-gearing or oversized motors that were never actually needed. See formula-kt-from-kv.

2. Assuming all available motor torque converts into acceleration or pushing force. For a 454 g robot, wheel-to-floor traction is almost always the real limit, not motor torque. In the worked drivetrain example on this page, the drivetrain could theoretically deliver 20.0 N of driving force, but the traction limit (formula-traction-limited-force) was only 3.56 N -- meaning the wheels would spin long before the motor ran out of torque. Builders who chase higher-torque motors or lower gear ratios to "push harder" without also improving wheel grip are solving the wrong problem.

3. Treating total stored kinetic energy as the energy delivered in one hit. A weapon rarely gives up all its stored KE in a single strike; it slows down by only however much the impact actually resisted it, keeping the rest of its spin for the next hit. In the worked example, only about 19.8 J of a 71.3 J stored total was delivered in one hit (formula-energy-per-hit-vs-stored) -- using the full stored KE number to size structure or predict single-hit damage overstates the real per-hit force.

4. Ignoring gyroscopic precession torque when picking turn rate for a vertical spinner. Precession torque scales directly with weapon MOI, weapon speed, AND commanded turn rate (formula-gyroscopic-precession-torque); a chassis that seems perfectly stable standing still or driving straight can start to rock or tip the instant the driver spins it in place at full weapon speed. The worked example shows a case where the precession torque (0.204 Nm) barely exceeds the chassis's own tipping threshold (0.200 Nm) -- a small change in turn rate is the difference between stable and "gyro dance."

5. Sizing the ESC and motor from no-load rpm and stall current alone, without checking spin-up time or battery voltage sag under load. A motor's real available torque at the actual operating point is much lower than its stall torque, and battery voltage sags under load (formula-voltage-sag) right when full stall current is being demanded (weapon impacts, drivetrain stalls). Skipping this check leads to slower-than-expected spin-up, ESC brownouts under load, and weapon motors that seem "weaker than the spec sheet" mid-fight -- when the real cause is under-provisioned battery C-rating or ESC current headroom (formula-esc-current-headroom).
