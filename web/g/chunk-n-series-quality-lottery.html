N10/N20/N30 Gearmotors and the Quality-Lottery Problem

Drive Motors

antweightplastic-antweightdrive-motorn-seriesquality-lotterybudget

The N-series family — N10 (smallest, ~10mm), N20 (most common, 12mm), and N30 (largest, ~30mm) micro gearmotors — is the budget backbone of plastic antweight and hobby-grade drivetrains. These motors are sold by dozens of overlapping resellers on AliExpress, Amazon, and similar marketplaces, almost always originating from the same small set of OEM factories, in an enormous range of stock gear ratios: 20:1, 30:1, 50:1, 75:1, 100:1, 150:1, 210:1, 250:1, 298:1, 380:1, and 1000:1 are all commonly available, alongside 3V/6V/12V motor voltage options.

The base N20 motor (before gearing) runs at roughly 15,000 RPM no-load at 6V — this is corroborated by real product listings, e.g. a 298:1 N20 gearmotor advertised and sold specifically as running 50 RPM at 6V (15,000 / 298 ≈ 50, a clean match). Gearbox length is constant across most ratios (~9mm) except 1000:1 (and sometimes 380:1), which get a 3.5mm-longer, reinforced housing rated for roughly 25 kg·mm (2.5 kg-cm) of continuous output torque versus ~20 kg·mm (2.0 kg-cm) for the standard ratios — a purely mechanical strength limit on the gear teeth, independent of how much torque the motor could theoretically produce electrically at that ratio.

The real problem with this whole family isn't the published spec sheet — it's that the spec sheet often doesn't reflect what actually arrives. Listings frequently advertise "metal gear" motors that turn out to have only some gear stages in metal and the rest in plastic, and even within a single order, individual units can vary in winding quality, no-load speed, stall current, and outright dead-on-arrival rate. This inconsistency is widely known in the antweight community as the "quality lottery." It doesn't make N-series motors unusable — plenty of successful plastic antweights run on them — but it does change how a builder should treat them: buy spares from the start and treat them as a semi-consumable part, bench-test every single unit before final installation (measuring free-run current and no-load RPM and rejecting any outlier compared to its batch-mates), and avoid depending on an untested cheap motor as the sole drive for an important match. Builders who want to skip this risk entirely pay the premium for FingerTech, Repeat Robotics, or Pololu motors instead, all of which publish (and generally meet) a consistent, verifiable spec.
