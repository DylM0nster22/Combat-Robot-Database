Banned Weapons and Materials: What Every Major Org Agrees On

Safety & Banned Items

Despite differences in wording, weight classes, and judging systems, the major combat robotics rulesets (SPARC, NHRL, RoboGames, BattleBots, AWS/UK) converge on nearly the same core list of banned weapons and materials, because the underlying safety reasoning is identical everywhere: nothing that endangers spectators/officials beyond the arena barrier, nothing that permanently contaminates the arena surface for the next match, and nothing that makes damage impossible for judges/audiences to verify.

Liquids and fluids: banned almost everywhere, though NHRL's wording shows the two narrow exceptions most rulesets implicitly share: liquid expelled from the robot is banned generally, but liquid used purely as a fuel source is allowed, and liquid that turns gaseous shortly after leaving the robot (or before hitting the opponent) is also allowed — i.e. the ban targets a lasting liquid mess or a liquid weapon effect, not incidental fuel or something that evaporates before it can contaminate anything. AWS specifically calls out both 'fluid-based weapons' and separately bans batteries containing liquid acid or electrolyte.

Untethered and over-speed projectiles: NHRL puts a hard number on this where many rulesets only say 'no projectiles' — any projectile weapon is capped at 150 mph, and even a tethered projectile must not be designed in a way likely to become entangled with the opponent (blurring into the separate entanglement ban). NHRL defines entanglement itself precisely as any component 'designed to be entangled in the rotational or moving parts of the opponent,' a definition broad enough to cover string, tape, nets, or a deliberately snagging weapon geometry.

Fire and flame: banned by default everywhere, though a few large, heavily-supervised events (RoboGames, historically some BattleBots-adjacent exhibitions) allow specially pre-approved, licensed flamethrower weapons under strict extra safety conditions — this is very much the exception, not the rule, and never available at the antweight/beetleweight club level.

Entanglement: nets, string, tape, or any device meant to tangle an opponent's weapon or drivetrain is banned nearly everywhere (SPARC, RoboGames, AWS) because it can permanently disable a robot in a way that isn't 'damage' judges can score, and can be dangerous to retrieve after a match. A notable named exception: Germany's RoboGames-adjacent scene has historically permitted entanglement in a separate, clearly-labeled 'net-fighting' side bracket rather than mainstream weight-class combat.

RF jamming / EMP / electrical weapons: banned everywhere, since deliberately interfering with an opponent's radio control (rather than beating it in a fair fight) also risks jamming other robots' or the arena's own radio-linked safety systems — an inherently unacceptable risk at any event running multiple simultaneous transmitters.

Untethered projectiles: banned nearly universally (RoboGames explicitly, AWS via its tethered-weapon rule, SPARC generally) because anything that can leave the robot uncontrolled is an immediate spectator-safety hazard; where a detachable weapon component is allowed at all, it must remain tethered (AWS: 3 ft / 914 mm max).

Explosives and hazardous materials: universally banned, including flammable solids, and most rulesets also prohibit powders, sand, ball bearings, foams, and liquefied gases specifically because of arena-surface contamination risk (explicit in RoboGames' rules) rather than direct combat danger alone.

Materials at the plastic-class level: this is a narrower, class-specific restriction rather than a safety ban — plastic antweight/beetleweight forbid TPU, nylon, and fiber-reinforced composite filaments, not because they're dangerous, but to preserve the class's low-cost, low-damage design intent.

A builder moving between organizations should still read each event's current rulebook in full, since specific wording, exceptions (like Germany's net-fighting bracket), and edge cases (what counts as an 'approved' flamethrower, or exactly which magnetic effects are banned) do vary, but the underlying philosophy — no liquids, no uncontrolled fire, no entanglement, no jamming, no loose projectiles, no explosives — is close to universal across every major hobby ruleset in this research.
