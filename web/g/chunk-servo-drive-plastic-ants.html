Servo-Based Drive for Plastic Antweights

Drive Motors

Plastic antweights, built and raced on tight budgets and gram counts, have long used the cheapest, simplest drive mechanism available in the RC hobby world: the 9g micro servo, modified (or purchased pre-modified) for continuous rotation. A normal hobby servo takes a PWM pulse (typically 1-2ms) and moves its output shaft to a corresponding angle, using an internal potentiometer to sense and hold position via a small feedback control loop. Removing that positional feedback — physically pulling the potentiometer and replacing it with two fixed resistors set to the servo's centered voltage, and usually clipping a mechanical stop tab inside the gearbox — breaks the position-holding behavior and turns the same PWM signal into a direct speed-and-direction command instead: a 1ms pulse spins one direction, 2ms spins the other, and roughly 1.5ms (the old 'centered' position) stops the motor. The result behaves almost exactly like a small ESC-driven gearmotor from the transmitter's point of view, but needs zero separate ESC or motor driver hardware — the servo plugs straight into a receiver channel.

Builders who don't want to do the modification themselves can buy factory continuous-rotation servos (commonly referred to by part numbers like FS90R) that ship ready to use, with more consistent centering than a hand-modified unit, at a small price premium over a basic positional 9g servo.

The appeal for plastic antweights is straightforward: at roughly 9g each, two servos plus a receiver is often the lightest and cheapest complete drive system available, with no ESC weight or cost, no separate motor-driver wiring, and a huge amount of freely available documentation and tutorials for the modification. The trade-offs are real, though. Servo gearboxes are built from a stack of small plastic gears sized for holding a load still, not for absorbing repeated combat impacts, and the motor inside is sized for positioning torque rather than continuous drive torque — so top speed and pushing power both fall well short of what a dedicated gearmotor-plus-ESC combination of similar weight can deliver. In practice, servo drive suits beginner builds, very light or defensive plastic antweights that don't need to win pushing matches, and builds where simplicity of wiring and zero extra electronics genuinely outweighs raw performance. Builders chasing a competitive edge in the plastic antweight class typically graduate to N-series or Pololu gearmotors with a small ESC once they're ready to trade some of that simplicity for speed and torque.
