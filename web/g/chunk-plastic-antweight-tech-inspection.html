How Tech Inspection Verifies 'Plastic Only'

Rules

plastic-antweightrulesinspectionenforcement

Plastic ant technical inspection is, by design, lighter-weight than the pressure-vessel-certificate-checking inspection larger weight classes face for pneumatics/hydraulics — but it still has to answer one specific question with confidence: is the structural/armor/weapon material actually one of the approved plastics, and is it actually 3D printed rather than machined?

Visual inspection is the default and primary method. SCAR's Plastic Ant FAQ states plainly that 'datasheets are not required' for a normal build — an inspector looks at the surface finish (visible layer lines are a good sign it's genuinely printed rather than milled/cast), checks that fasteners are countersunk or shrouded, and looks for obviously non-plastic materials like exposed sheet metal, carbon fiber weave, or a suspiciously glossy/dense coating that might indicate an epoxy or resin reinforcement layer.

Suspicious builds get closer scrutiny. If a part looks implausibly rigid, dense, or smooth for its stated material (for example, a 'PETG' panel that doesn't flex at all when pressed, or a part that feels far heavier than its printed volume should), inspectors can investigate further — this might mean asking the builder directly what filament and settings were used, requesting to see other parts printed from the same spool, or in rare cases asking to cut into or otherwise verify a part before allowing the robot to compete.

Countersinking/shrouding is checked physically, not just declared: Robot Ruckus's specific wording (screws 'should be countersunk or shrouded') implies an inspector will run a finger or eye over the outer shell looking for proud screw heads that could act as tiny armor studs or catch points.

Annealed/acid-smoothed parts are pre-approved as a category (per SCAR's FAQ) so builders don't need to disclose post-processing that doesn't add foreign material — the rule targets material additives, coatings like Bondo/epoxy, and reinforcement layers, not heat or solvent treatments of the plastic itself.

No destructive testing is standard. Because plastic ant inspection relies on visual/tactile checks and builder honesty backed by the threat of disqualification 'at the Event Organizer's discretion' for violating 'the spirit of the class' (SPARC 4.4.3), the system depends heavily on community trust and the fact that most violations (like a hidden carbon insert) would be visible on close inspection or would show up as an implausible durability advantage in the actual matches, which draws scrutiny from competitors and organizers alike.
