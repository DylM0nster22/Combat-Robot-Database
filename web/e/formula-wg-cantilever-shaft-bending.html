Cantilevered shaft bending stress under impact load

A weapon shaft supported by only one bearing (cantilevered) sees bending stress proportional to the impact force times the unsupported length; shortening that length or adding a second support point cuts stress dramatically and is the main structural argument for double-bearing shaft support.

Expression

M = F_impact * L;  sigma = M*c / I_section, with I_section = pi*d^4/64 for a solid round shaft

Variables

F_impactestimated peak impact force during a hit, N
Lunsupported cantilever length from the bearing to the load point, m
dshaft diameter, m
cdistance from neutral axis to outer fiber = d/2, m
I_sectionsecond moment of area of the shaft cross-section, m^4
sigmabending stress in the shaft, Pa

Worked example

6 mm diameter shaft, an estimated 1000 N instantaneous impact spike, 10 mm of unsupported cantilever length between the single bearing and the weapon: I_section = pi0.006^4/64 = 6.36e-11 m^4; M = 10000.010 = 10 Nm; sigma = 100.003/6.36e-11 = 4.72e8 Pa = 472 MPa -- enough to permanently bend a mild-steel shaft (yield ~250 MPa) but survivable for a hardened alloy shaft. Adding a second bearing close to the weapon face to cut the effective cantilever to 3 mm drops the moment to 3 N*m and the stress to about 141 MPa, well under yield for almost any shaft material. The 1000 N figure is a rough estimate for illustration -- real impact loads vary hugely with weapon mass, rpm, and what is struck -- but the L-scaling relationship is the key structural takeaway.
