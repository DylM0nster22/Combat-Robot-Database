Required Motor Torque for a Target Acceleration / Pushing Force

Sizes the motor-shaft stall torque needed to accelerate a given robot mass at a target rate (or to sustain a target pushing force), working back through the wheel radius, gear ratio, and gearbox efficiency.

Formulaantweightplastic-antweightformulatorqueaccelerationgear-ratio

Expression

F = m * a;  T_wheel = F * r / n_driven_wheels;  T_motor_stall_required = T_wheel / (gear_ratio * efficiency)

Variables

mrobot mass, kg
atarget acceleration or deceleration, m/s^2 (e.g. 1-2g for a fast start/ram)
Ftotal force required, Newtons
rwheel radius, meters
n_driven_wheelsnumber of powered wheels sharing the load
T_wheelrequired torque at the wheel/output shaft, N*m
gear_ratiogearbox reduction, N:1 (use N)
efficiencygearbox mechanical efficiency, ~0.65-0.80 for spur gearboxes, ~0.80-0.92 for planetary
T_motor_stall_requiredminimum motor-shaft stall torque needed, N*m (convert to kg-cm by dividing N*m by 0.09807)

Worked example

1 lb robot (m = 0.4536 kg) targeting 2g acceleration (a = 19.6 m/s^2) on two driven wheels of radius 0.019 m (1.5in diameter): F = 0.4536 x 19.6 = 8.89 N. T_wheel per side = 8.89 x 0.019 / 2 = 0.0844 N*m = 0.86 kg-cm. With a 33.3:1 Silver Spark gearbox at ~0.75 efficiency: T_motor_stall_required = 0.86 / (33.3 x 0.75) = 0.0345 kg-cm, comfortably under the Silver Spark's ~0.046 kg-cm bare-motor stall torque, so this ratio has margin to spare for a 2g start. At the fast 11.1:1 ratio, the same math needs 0.86 / (11.1 x 0.75) = 0.103 kg-cm at the motor shaft — more than the bare motor's stall torque can supply, meaning 11.1:1 alone cannot sustain a full 2g acceleration at stall (though brief transient acceleration before the motor reaches that speed is a separate, more forgiving calculation).
