Traction Limit: Why Torque Beyond Available Grip Is Wasted

Defines the maximum pushing/accelerating force a drivetrain can actually put to the ground, set by wheel-to-floor friction rather than motor torque; any drivetrain torque capacity beyond this ceiling just spins the wheels and wastes battery/heat instead of producing more force.

Formulaantweightplastic-antweightformulatractionfrictionwheel-slip

Expression

F_max = mu * N

Variables

mucoefficient of friction between the wheel tread and the arena floor (dimensionless; roughly 0.3-0.5 for bare foam on polycarbonate, 0.5-0.8 for silicone/compliant rubber tread on polycarbonate or wood)
Nnormal force pressing the driven wheels into the floor, Newtons (approximately the full robot weight for a 2WD antweight with no significant weight on non-driven casters/skids)
F_maxmaximum pushing/traction force before the wheels slip, Newtons

Worked example

1 lb robot (weight = 0.4536 kg x 9.81 = 4.45 N) on silicone-tread wheels with mu ~= 0.6 on a polycarbonate floor: F_max = 0.6 x 4.45 = 2.67 N. Compare this to the pushing force available at the wheel from formula-required-torque-acceleration-pushing: even the lowest-torque stock Silver Spark ratio (11.1:1, ~0.38 kg-cm output stall torque, ~0.0373 N*m) on a 0.019 m wheel radius over 2 driven wheels can produce up to 2 x 0.0373 / 0.019 = 3.9 N of force at the wheel before the motor itself stalls — already above the 2.67 N traction ceiling. In other words, the wheels slip before the motor runs out of torque, so choosing a slower/higher-torque ratio than 11.1:1 purely to 'push harder' would not actually increase the robot's real-world pushing force; it would only reduce top speed. Common guidance (e.g. from Just 'Cuz Robotics) is to size the gear ratio with roughly 1.5-2x the torque strictly required to reach the traction limit, then optimize the remaining ratio choice for speed and control feel.
