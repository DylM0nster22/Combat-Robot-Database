Stall Current vs ESC Rating and Duty-Cycle Thermal Limits

Explains how to size a drive ESC/driver's continuous and burst current ratings against a motor's stall current, and why sustained full-stall (e.g. pinned against a wall or opponent) is a thermal, time-limited event rather than something a small combat robot motor can hold indefinitely.

Expression

I_stall_total = n_motors * I_stall_per_motor;  esc_continuous_rating >= I_cruise_expected;  esc_burst_rating >= I_stall_total (with margin);  t_safe_stall is inversely related to I^2 (I^2*R resistive heating)

Variables

I_stall_per_motorstall current of one drive motor at the operating voltage, Amps
n_motorsnumber of drive motors on that ESC/battery branch
I_stall_totalworst-case simultaneous stall current draw, Amps
I_cruise_expectedtypical current draw while driving normally (well below stall), Amps
esc_continuous_ratingESC/driver's continuous current rating, Amps
esc_burst_ratingESC/driver's short-burst peak current rating, Amps
t_safe_stallapproximate time a motor can be safely held at stall before winding damage from resistive heating

Worked example

Two FingerTech Silver Sparks, each 1.3A stall at 7.4V: I_stall_total = 2 x 1.3 = 2.6A combined worst case (both wheels pinned simultaneously). Normal cruising current is much lower, roughly 0.2-0.6A per motor. A drive ESC/driver should have a continuous rating comfortably above the cruising current (so it runs cool during normal driving) and a burst rating at or above the 1.3A per-channel stall figure with headroom (e.g. a 2-3A continuous per-channel rating is typical for antweight brushed ESCs). Because these small motors have very little thermal mass, holding full stall (e.g. wedged against an arena wall) for more than roughly 10-20 seconds risks overheating the winding insulation even though the ESC itself may tolerate the current — drivers are taught to 'bump' the throttle in short pulses rather than holding full stall continuously in a pushing match.
