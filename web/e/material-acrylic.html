Acrylic (PMMA)

Cast or extruded polymethyl methacrylate — decent tensile strength and very stiff/scratch-resistant, but a well-known trap for combat robot armor because it is extremely brittle under impact loading despite looking strong on a datasheet.

Materialantweightplastic-antweighttrap-materialbrittlebudget

Strengths

  • Cheap, widely available, laser-cuttable
  • Good tensile strength and stiffness on paper
  • Scratch resistant, looks clean

Weaknesses

  • Extremely low elongation-to-break (~4%) — essentially zero ductility, so it cannot absorb impact energy by deforming
  • Shatters into sharp shards under a hard or even moderate impact — actively dangerous in an arena
  • Laser-cut edges (common hobbyist fabrication method) leave micro-fractures that make cracking under load even more likely
  • No meaningful notch or impact toughness

Notes

Acrylic is the textbook "the trap" material in combat robotics: its datasheet tensile strength (~70 MPa) looks comparable to nylon or polycarbonate, which misleads beginners into using it as armor. But tensile strength measured in a slow pull test says almost nothing about impact toughness — acrylic has essentially no ability to yield plastically, so all impact energy goes straight into brittle crack propagation. A polycarbonate panel of the same thickness will flex and absorb a hit; an acrylic panel of the same thickness will crack and often explode into shards. Most build guides and forums explicitly warn new builders away from acrylic armor for exactly this reason — it is fine for a display stand or a non-combat cover, never for anything that will be hit.
