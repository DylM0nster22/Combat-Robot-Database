Torque and traction required to climb an opponent's wedge

Two separate conditions must both hold to drive up onto an opponent's wedge at angle theta: (1) the drivetrain must supply enough torque at the wheel, and (2) the wheels must have enough traction against the now-tilted (and often slippery) wedge surface -- whichever is smaller sets the actual climbable angle.

Formulaantweightplastic-antweightdrivetraintractionwedgeincline

Expression

F_needed = m_robot * g * sin(theta) ; T_needed_at_wheel = F_needed * r_wheel / eta ; traction_ok if mu * m_robot * g * cos(theta) >= F_needed

Variables

F_neededforce needed up the slope to overcome gravity component, N (ignoring rolling resistance)
m_robottotal robot mass, kg
thetawedge angle from horizontal, rad
T_needed_at_wheeloutput torque needed at the wheel to supply F_needed, N*m
r_wheelwheel radius, m
etadrivetrain efficiency, dimensionless
muwheel-to-wedge-surface friction coefficient, dimensionless

Worked example

m_robot = 0.454 kg, theta = 15 deg (0.2618 rad), r_wheel = 0.016 m, eta = 0.80, mu = 0.8 (assume similar grip on the wedge surface). F_needed = 0.454 9.81 sin(15deg) = 4.453 0.2588 = 1.152 N. T_needed_at_wheel = 1.152 0.016 / 0.80 = 0.01843 / 0.80 = 0.02304 Nm -- well inside the drivetrain's available 0.16 Nm per wheel from formula-acceleration-from-torque, so torque is not the bottleneck. Traction check: mu m_robot g cos(theta) = 0.8 4.453 cos(15deg) = 3.562 0.966 = 3.44 N, which is far more than the 1.152 N needed, so this 15 degree wedge is climbable. Solving tan(theta_max) = mu gives theta_max = atan(0.8) = 38.7 deg as the traction-limited steepest wedge this robot could climb at all (before its own weight component alone overcomes available friction), independent of motor torque.
