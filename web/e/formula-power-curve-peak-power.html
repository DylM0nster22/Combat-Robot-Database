DC motor power curve and peak power at half stall speed

For an ideal DC (brushed or BLDC-as-DC-equivalent) motor, torque falls off linearly with speed from T_stall at 0 rpm to 0 at no-load rpm. Multiplying torque by speed gives a power curve that peaks exactly halfway between (at half the no-load speed and half the stall torque), which is the operating point a weapon or drivetrain wants to sit near for maximum mechanical power.

Expression

T(omega) = T_stall * (1 - omega/omega_no_load) ; P(omega) = T(omega) * omega ; P_max = T_stall * omega_no_load / 4 at omega = omega_no_load / 2

Variables

T(omega)torque at angular velocity omega, N*m
T_stallstall torque, N*m
omegaangular velocity, rad/s
omega_no_loadno-load angular velocity, rad/s
P(omega)mechanical power at that angular velocity, W
P_maxpeak mechanical power, W (occurs at omega_no_load/2)

Worked example

T_stall = 0.1042 Nm, omega_no_load = 3196 rad/s. Peak power point: omega = 3196/2 = 1598 rad/s (15,262 rpm), T there = 0.1042/2 = 0.0521 Nm. P_max = T_stall omega_no_load / 4 = 0.1042 3196 / 4 = 333.2 / 4 = 83.3 W. At the actual 20,000 rpm (2094.4 rad/s) operating point used elsewhere on this page (past the peak-power point, on the higher-speed/lower-torque side): T = 0.1042(1-2094.4/3196) = 0.03595 Nm, P = 0.03595*2094.4 = 75.3 W -- slightly below the 83.3 W peak, consistent with running a bit faster than the ideal peak-power point.
